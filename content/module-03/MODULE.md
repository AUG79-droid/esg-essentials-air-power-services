# Module 03 — Ratings, Reporting & Greenwashing

## Module opening

Two ESG rating providers assess the same company. One gives a strong result; the other is cautious. The company reports a reduction against target, but the boundary excludes most suppliers. Its product is labeled “responsible.” Which signal should you trust?

Trust is not binary. Reporting, ratings and labels compress complex evidence for decisions. They can improve transparency, but only when the user understands their purpose, boundary and limitations.

**Expected learning time:** 48 minutes

### Learning objectives

You will explain why organizations report; distinguish disclosure from performance; describe how ratings work and why providers disagree; evaluate boundaries, methods and data quality; separate claim, evidence, metric, target and outcome; and detect common greenwashing patterns.

## Lesson 1 — Why ESG reporting exists

Reporting makes selected information available to management, investors, customers, regulators, employees and other stakeholders. Internally, a reporting process can clarify ownership, expose missing data and support decisions. Externally, it can improve accountability and comparability.

Disclosure is not performance. A company can report comprehensively about a serious impact without having resolved it. Another may perform reasonably but report weakly. Good reporting helps a user understand both outcome and management; it should not be mistaken for the outcome itself.

A useful disclosure explains the boundary, material issues, methods, measures, governance, actions, progress and limitations. It distinguishes observed data from estimates and ambition from achievement. It also states the reporting period and whether information has been reviewed or assured.

The source refers to sustainability frameworks and increasing disclosure requirements. IFRS S1 and S2 were issued in June 2023 and became effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024, subject to adoption in each jurisdiction. Targeted amendments to IFRS S2 issued in December 2025 apply from 1 January 2027, with earlier application permitted. GRI’s revised Universal Standards apply to reporting from 1 January 2023. In the EU, the CSRD timetable was postponed and amended in 2025–2026, and the Commission adopted revised ESRS on 3 July 2026; the legal effect of a delegated act depends on completion of scrutiny and Official Journal publication. Applicability, interoperability, assurance, digital tagging and transition relief must therefore be determined for the entity, reporting period and jurisdiction—not taught as universal.

## Lesson 2 — Claim, evidence, metric, target and outcome

A **claim** is what an organization says: “this process is lower carbon.” **Evidence** is what supports or challenges that statement: activity data, method, records and review. A **metric** is a defined measure: kilograms of greenhouse gas per repaired unit within a stated boundary. A **target** is a desired future level and date. An **outcome** is the change that actually occurred.

Confusion among these categories is a common source of weak reasoning. A target is not an outcome. A metric is not evidence unless populated with reliable data. Evidence of activity—training delivered, audits completed, money allocated—does not automatically prove an environmental or social result.

An evidence-conscious user asks: What exactly is claimed? Compared with what? Which lifecycle stages and entities are included? What method and data are used? Who owns and checks them? What uncertainty or adverse effect is omitted?

## Lesson 3 — How ratings work

An ESG rating usually converts many observations into a smaller score or category. A provider selects issues, gathers data, normalizes indicators, applies weights, assesses management or exposure and aggregates results. Some indicators are quantitative, such as emissions or water withdrawal. Others require judgment, such as board expertise or policy quality.

Ratings may be absolute or relative to peers. A peer-relative result can identify a company assessed as stronger within its sector; it does not mean the sector or company has low impact. Ratings can also have different purposes. One may focus on financially material risk to enterprise value; another may emphasize impacts on society and environment.

The MSCI interviews describe a provider approach using public data, industry-relevant issues and peer comparison at the time of recording. That is a practitioner example, not a current methodology statement. Provider methodology, scale, issue selection, controversy treatment and data sources can change and must be obtained from the provider’s current published methodology before operational use.

## Lesson 4 — Why ratings disagree

Providers can disagree because they measure different things, use different boundaries, select different indicators, assign different weights, use different data or update at different times. They may treat controversies, missing data and company policies differently.

Divergence does not prove ratings are useless. It means the user must understand the question each rating answers. A credit rating, product review and safety inspection can also differ because they examine different constructs. The appropriate response is to inspect methodology and avoid combining scores as if they were interchangeable.

In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2024/3005 establishes authorization, organizational, transparency and conflicts-of-interest requirements for ESG rating providers, with ESMA oversight and detailed 2026 implementing measures. It does not make all ratings comparable or remove methodological judgment. Organizations must still check territorial scope, exclusions, transition provisions and any other applicable jurisdiction’s rules.

## Lesson 5 — Data quality and boundaries

Data quality includes accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness and relevance. Traceability matters: can the reported number be connected to original records, transformations, assumptions and approval? Controls matter too: who enters data, who reviews it, how changes are logged and how errors are corrected?

Boundary choices can create a technically correct but misleading impression. A waste figure may exclude contractors; an emissions measure may cover only owned operations; a social metric may average away a high-risk region; an improvement may result from selling an asset rather than changing performance.

### From a dashboard number to governed evidence

Use the evidence chain **source → owner → definition → boundary → method → calculation → control → review → change history**.

- **Source:** identify the originating system, record, measurement, supplier evidence or estimate.
- **Owner:** assign responsibility for the data and authority to correct it.
- **Definition and boundary:** state exactly what the indicator means, which entities, sites, people, activities and period it includes, and exclusions.
- **Method and calculation:** preserve units, factors, formulas, assumptions, allocation and estimation method so another competent person can reproduce the result.
- **Control:** specify checks for completeness, accuracy, authorization, duplication, unusual values and access.
- **Review:** record who challenged the result, exceptions found and how they were resolved.
- **Change history:** retain versions, base-year changes, restatements and reasons. Do not overwrite the audit trail.

Good data can include estimates. The requirement is not false precision: label estimation, explain uncertainty and use a method proportionate to the decision. Consistency supports comparison, but a method should change when it is wrong or no longer fit for purpose; document the effect rather than silently rewriting history. A dashboard number without provenance is weak evidence because users cannot test what it represents or reproduce it.

### Assurance readiness is preparation, not assurance

Before an assurance engagement, the reporting team should have defined criteria and boundary, documented methods and judgments, retained evidence, operated controls, reviewed exceptions, reconciled versions and corrected known errors. It should be able to reproduce material calculations and explain management judgments or representations where relevant.

These preparations do not guarantee an assurance conclusion. **Internal review** is management or peer challenge. **Internal audit** provides independent assurance within the organization under its mandate. **Verification** checks specified information or performance against defined requirements. **Certification** attests conformity to the requirements of a certification scheme or standard. **External assurance** is an independent engagement resulting in a formal conclusion under defined criteria and an assurance standard. Scope, competence, independence and level of work differ.

ISSA 5000 provides a global principles-based standard for sustainability assurance engagements and can support limited or reasonable assurance, subject to adoption and engagement terms. Completing this lesson neither selects that standard nor turns preparers into assurance practitioners.

**EXPLORE FURTHER — Assurance engagements:** specialists determine suitable criteria, materiality, engagement risk, evidence procedures and reporting. Learners should focus on producing traceable information and responding transparently to limitations and errors.

## Lesson 6 — Greenwashing

Greenwashing is the misrepresentation or exaggeration of environmental credentials. Related “washing” can occur for social or broader sustainability claims. The source distinguishes misleading characterization from outright fraud: a nonexistent project is fraudulent regardless of whether it is called green; an existing project can still be misleadingly characterized.

Common patterns include:

- vague terms with no defined criterion;
- selective disclosure of a small benefit while omitting a larger burden;
- an irrelevant claim that describes what is already required or normal;
- an unsupported target with no plan, boundary or interim evidence;
- a label treated as proof of outcome;
- boundary shifting between periods or products;
- impressive precision built on weak estimates;
- imagery that implies environmental benefit without evidence.

Greenwashing detection should not become automatic disbelief. The goal is calibrated confidence: identify what would substantiate the claim and whether the remaining uncertainty matters to the decision.

Advertising, consumer, competition, financial-product and corporate-reporting rules may all apply to a sustainability claim. The legal test and enforcement authority depend on the jurisdiction, audience and product. In the EU financial sector, examples include the Taxonomy framework, the European Green Bond regime and ESMA’s fund-name guidelines; none substitutes for a claim-by-claim legal assessment.

## What this means in Air Power Services

### AIR POWER SERVICES LEARNING SCENARIO — Three claims under review

1. “Low-carbon repair” is supported by facility electricity data but excludes transport, replacement parts and rework.
2. “Responsibly sourced alloy” is supported by a tier-one certificate with no sub-tier scope.
3. “Zero-waste maintenance line” means no waste sent directly to landfill, but some waste is transferred to an intermediary with unknown treatment.

None is automatically false. Each requires a defined claim, comparison, boundary, method, evidence and outcome. The reviewer should narrow or qualify a claim when evidence supports only part of it.

The lesson is not to accept or reject ratings as a class; it is to use them according to purpose and methodology.

## Myth vs reality

### Myth 1: “More disclosure means better performance.”

**Reality:** Disclosure makes information visible. Performance depends on actual conditions and outcomes.

### Myth 2: “Different ratings mean one provider is dishonest.”

**Reality:** Different scope, purpose, weights and data can produce different results. Methodology inspection is required.

### Myth 3: “A claim is safe if every sentence is technically true.”

**Reality:** Omission, visual implication, boundary choice and comparison can make a technically accurate fragment misleading overall.

## Decision lens

1. What exact claim or decision is this information intended to support?
2. Is it a claim, evidence item, metric, target or observed outcome?
3. What boundary, baseline, method, period and comparison apply?
4. What data are measured, estimated or missing?
5. Who owns, controls and reviews the information?
6. What does the rating/report/label not measure?
7. Could selective presentation or imagery create a broader impression than evidence supports?
8. What qualification, additional evidence or escalation is proportionate?

## Module close

Reporting and ratings are tools for reducing information complexity. They become dangerous when users treat them as proof detached from purpose, method and boundary.

### Key takeaways

- Disclosure and performance are different.
- Claims, metrics, targets, evidence and outcomes must be kept separate.
- Ratings reflect models and may legitimately disagree.
- Data lineage and boundary choices shape credibility.
- Anti-greenwashing practice replaces vague confidence with testable evidence.
