# Module 01 — Environmental Essentials

## Module opening

### The dilemma

A maintenance team can extend the life of an existing component, replace it with a lighter design or establish a remanufacturing route. One option uses less energy in service; another avoids new material extraction; another improves availability. Which is the “environmental” choice?

The question is deliberately incomplete. Environmental decisions involve systems, boundaries and trade-offs. A benefit in one stage can create a burden elsewhere. This module develops the concepts needed to see those connections without implying that an aerospace product is environmentally beneficial by nature.

**Expected learning time:** 66 minutes

### Learning objectives

You will be able to explain climate stability and emissions concepts; identify dependencies and impacts involving energy, water, land, materials and biodiversity; describe pollution, waste and circularity; trace environmental issues into business risk; and recognize the limits of environmental evidence.

## Lesson 1 — Climate stability and business exposure

Climate describes long-term patterns in temperature, rainfall, wind and other conditions. Climate stability does not mean an unchanging atmosphere; it means a system whose ranges and patterns are sufficiently stable for ecological and human systems to adapt. The IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report synthesis states that human activities, principally through greenhouse-gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming; global surface temperature was about 1.1°C above 1850–1900 in 2011–2020. This is the assessment basis used here, not a real-time temperature claim.

Greenhouse gases retain heat in the atmosphere. Increasing their concentration changes average conditions and the likelihood or severity of hazards. For an organization, the useful distinction is between **mitigation** and **adaptation**. Mitigation reduces the drivers of climate change, for example by cutting emissions. Adaptation reduces vulnerability or exposure to effects that occur, for example by improving cooling, drainage, supply redundancy or emergency planning.

Environmental hazards become business risks through a pathway. A hazard such as extreme heat matters when a facility, workforce or supplier is exposed and vulnerable. Consequences can include interrupted operations, reduced equipment efficiency, unsafe working conditions, higher cost, asset damage or unavailable transport. Climate risk is therefore not only “a weather issue.” It intersects with asset design, operations, people and supply chains.

### A beginner method for physical climate risk and adaptation

Use the chain **hazard → exposure → vulnerability → impact → adaptation response → residual risk**.

1. **Define the decision and time horizon.** A facility plan, supplier decision and short maintenance window need different evidence and horizons.
2. **Screen hazards.** Consider acute events such as flood, storm or heatwave and chronic shifts such as higher average temperature or declining water availability. Use credible location-specific information; a global average is not a site assessment.
3. **Map exposure.** Identify the people, assets, infrastructure, logistics routes, utilities and suppliers present where and when the hazard could occur.
4. **Assess vulnerability.** Ask how sensitive each exposed element is and what capacity it has to cope or adapt. The same heat hazard can affect a cooled facility and an exposed work area differently.
5. **Describe impacts and connections.** Trace operational, workforce, environmental, supply, cost and recovery consequences. Record dependencies and possible compound events.
6. **Develop responses.** Avoid exposure where feasible; reduce vulnerability through design, maintenance, redundancy, procedures, skills or contingency arrangements; and prepare response and recovery.
7. **Evaluate residual risk.** Adaptation reduces risk but rarely removes it. It can also shift risk to another location or group, so test trade-offs.
8. **Monitor assumptions and triggers.** Assign owners, indicators and review points for changing hazard information, asset condition, supplier location and response effectiveness.

A scenario is a coherent “what if,” not a prediction. Use more than one plausible future when uncertainty could change the decision, state assumptions and test whether the response remains useful across the range. Specialist climate science, engineering, safety and continuity support is required when screening could affect design bases, asset integrity or emergency planning.

## Lesson 2 — Emissions: what the numbers describe

An emissions inventory organizes greenhouse gases associated with an organization. The supplied material introduces the GHG Protocol’s familiar three-scope model. Scope 1 covers direct emissions from sources owned or controlled by the organization. Scope 2 covers indirect emissions associated with purchased energy. Scope 3 covers other value-chain emissions upstream and downstream.

The scopes describe where emissions sit relative to an organizational boundary; they do not rank moral importance. Scope 3 can be large and difficult to estimate because it depends on supplier, logistics, use-phase and end-of-life information. Scope 1 data may be closer to operational control but can still contain missing meters, inconsistent factors or boundary errors.

Every total rests on choices: which entities and operations are included, what period is measured, which activity data and emission factors are used, how estimates are treated and whether a base year has changed. A polished chart cannot compensate for an unclear boundary. Comparisons are meaningful only when methods and context are sufficiently aligned.

Targets add more choices. An absolute target concerns total emissions; an intensity target expresses emissions per unit of activity. Either can inform decisions, but an intensity improvement can coexist with a rise in total emissions. A target also needs a baseline, time horizon, scope, actions, accountable owner and progress method. As at 29 August 2026, the GHG Protocol Corporate Standard and Scope 2 and Scope 3 guidance remain the principal referenced accounting documents while the Corporate Suite is being updated. SBTi’s Corporate Net-Zero Standard v1.3.1 remains available for 2026 target setting; version 2.0 was published in June 2026 and validation against it is scheduled to open in 2027. Neither framework is, by itself, a universal legal disclosure requirement.

## Lesson 3 — Dependencies, impacts and natural resources

Organizations depend on environmental systems for energy, water, materials, land, stable operating conditions and ecosystem services. They also affect those systems through extraction, emissions, pollution, land conversion and waste. The same subject can be both dependency and impact.

Water illustrates the two directions. A facility or supplier may depend on reliable water for cooling, cleaning or processing. Regional scarcity can disrupt activity or increase cost. At the same time, withdrawal and discharge can affect other users and ecosystems. A global water total may hide location-specific stress, seasonality and quality. The course therefore uses site- and basin-specific evidence rather than carrying forward the transcript’s untraceable global water, wastewater, ocean, sea-level or economic figures.

Land and soil support agriculture, infrastructure and carbon storage. Degradation can reduce productivity and resilience. Forests store carbon, influence water cycles, provide habitats and support livelihoods. Clearing can therefore produce linked climate, biodiversity, water and community effects. Rock and mineral extraction supplies essential industrial inputs but can involve habitat disruption, pollution, energy use and impacts on nearby communities.

The correct conclusion is not that resources should never be used. It is that resource decisions need information about source, scarcity, extraction and processing, alternatives, efficiency, recovery and consequences for people and ecosystems.

## Lesson 4 — Energy and transition trade-offs

Fossil fuels have supported industrial systems because they are energy-dense and supported by extensive infrastructure. Their combustion is also a major source of greenhouse gases and air pollution. Renewable sources can reduce operational emissions but have constraints involving intermittency, infrastructure, storage, land, materials and local ecological effects.

Environmental reasoning therefore avoids two errors: treating current energy systems as consequence-free, and treating every low-carbon technology as impact-free. A decision should compare feasible options across an appropriate lifecycle and operational context. Reliability, safety and readiness remain essential constraints.

The course does not carry forward the transcript’s time-sensitive projections for renewable shares, investment needs or target years. Learners should use the latest relevant energy-system scenario, identify its publisher and assumptions, and distinguish a forecast from a policy target or organizational commitment.

## Lesson 5 — Biodiversity and nature

Biodiversity describes variation within species, between species and across ecosystems. It supports services such as pollination, water regulation, soil formation and resilience. The source identifies major drivers of loss: habitat change, climate change, pollution, invasive species, overexploitation and disease.

Nature-related risk is often location-specific. The same volume of water use or land disturbance can have different consequences depending on ecological sensitivity and cumulative pressure. A company may be distant from the visible impact while still connected through materials or suppliers.

TNFD’s September 2023 Recommendations provide a voluntary framework for identifying, assessing, managing and disclosing nature-related dependencies, impacts, risks and opportunities. TNFD’s LEAP approach is optional implementation guidance, not a certification or a universal legal requirement. A jurisdiction or reporting standard may refer to nature-related disclosure separately, so applicability must be checked rather than inferred from TNFD adoption.

### A beginner nature-risk screen

Nature risk is broader than counting species. Start with the decision and use four connected questions:

- **Dependency:** Which ecosystem services does the activity rely on—water flow and quality, soil stability, flood regulation, pollination, climate regulation or material provision?
- **Impact:** How could the activity change the state of nature through land or sea use, resource extraction, pollution, disturbance or invasive species?
- **Risk:** How could a dependency fail or an impact create operational, legal, financial, social or reputational consequences?
- **Opportunity:** Could avoidance, restoration, more resilient sourcing, circular design or collaboration reduce impact or dependency risk?

TNFD’s LEAP logic offers a useful sequence: **Locate** interfaces with nature, **Evaluate** dependencies and impacts, **Assess** risks and opportunities, and **Prepare** responses and relevant disclosure. At essentials level, use it as a screen, not as a claim that a formal TNFD assessment has been completed. Location matters because ecosystem condition, water basin, protected status, affected rights-holders and cumulative pressure differ by place.

For negative impacts, apply a mitigation hierarchy: first seek to avoid; then minimise what cannot be avoided; restore affected systems where feasible; and consider offsets or compensation only for residual impacts and only under appropriate specialist governance. Never treat compensation as permission to skip avoidance or mandatory controls.

## Lesson 6 — Pollution

Pollution occurs when substances or energy enter air, water or land at levels that harm people or ecosystems. Sources may be direct, such as combustion or discharge, or distributed across a value chain. The source connects air pollution to human health and describes water contamination, agricultural runoff, plastics, textile dyeing and mining impacts.

Control begins by understanding the pathway: source, release, transport, receptor and consequence. Preventing generation is generally stronger than managing pollution after release, but the feasible control depends on technical requirements and risk. Compliance limits are important; they do not by themselves establish that every material impact is understood.

The transcript’s health-burden figures and Jakarta litigation details are not carried forward because their exact source, date and procedural status were not established. Applicable pollution limits, permits and enforcement rules must be checked for the relevant substance, activity and jurisdiction.

For a durable pollution decision, use this hierarchy:

1. identify the substance or physical hazard and its technical function;
2. map where exposure or release could occur—air, water, soil, workplace, storage, transport, use or end of life;
3. identify people, ecosystems and assets that could be exposed;
4. confirm legal, safety, airworthiness, security and technical controls;
5. examine whether the function can be removed or delivered by a safer technically feasible alternative;
6. where substitution is not qualified, reduce risk through containment, closed process, ventilation, treatment, handling and maintenance controls;
7. monitor conditions and releases, retain evidence and define emergency/corrective action;
8. revisit the decision when hazard, exposure, technology or qualification evidence changes.

Substitution is not a simple “less hazardous” label. ECHA advises comparing hazard and exposure, technical performance, economic feasibility and wider lifecycle effects to avoid regrettable substitution. In APS examples, ESG preference never authorises an unqualified material or process and never overrides safety, airworthiness, security or mandatory technical authority.

## Lesson 7 — Waste and circularity

A linear model extracts resources, makes products, uses them and discards them. A circular model tries to preserve the value of products, components and materials through reduction, durability, reuse, repair, refurbishment, remanufacture and recycling. The higher-value loops often preserve more embedded work and material than breaking an item down for recycling.

Circularity is not automatically beneficial. A reusable system may require cleaning and transport. A lighter material may be harder to repair. Recycling can consume energy and yield lower-quality material. A component kept in service must remain safe and reliable. The relevant question is which feasible option reduces total burden without shifting unacceptable harm.

The source’s material examples demonstrate these trade-offs: plastic is durable and light but persistent; glass is recyclable but heavy and energy-intensive; metals retain value but extraction can be damaging; paper depends on forests and contamination affects recovery; textiles combine water, chemical and microplastic issues; food waste loses the resources embedded in production.

The transcript’s quantitative claims about waste, recycling, extraction, methane and circular-economy benefits are not carried forward without a named dataset, geography, year and boundary.

### Life-cycle thinking is not a formal LCA

**Life-cycle thinking** is the discipline of checking whether a decision shifts burden between extraction, manufacture, transport, use, maintenance and end of life—or between climate, toxicity, water, resource and other impact categories. It improves everyday questions but does not produce an ISO-compliant result.

A **formal life-cycle assessment (LCA)** defines a goal and scope, builds an inventory of relevant inputs and outputs, evaluates potential impact categories and interprets results. Sound comparison requires:

- a **functional unit** or comparison basis describing the service, quantity, quality and duration being compared;
- a **system boundary** showing included and excluded stages and processes;
- inventory data for materials, energy, emissions and waste related to that unit;
- relevant impact categories rather than one convenient score;
- an allocation rule where one process produces several products or functions;
- documented assumptions, data quality, gaps, variability and uncertainty;
- interpretation using completeness, consistency and sensitivity checks.

### AIR POWER SERVICES LEARNING SCENARIO — Repair versus replacement

A repair uses extra transport and treatment but avoids manufacturing a replacement. A lighter replacement may reduce use-stage energy but require new material and qualification work. The comparison first defines the service—providing the required function for a defined period—then draws both boundaries. A result limited to workshop energy cannot prove the lower total lifecycle impact. If assumptions about remaining life or use intensity reverse the preference, the recommendation must show that sensitivity.

**EXPLORE FURTHER — Formal LCA:** ISO 14040/14044 and the European Commission Environmental Footprint methods add detailed modelling, allocation, impact assessment, review and reporting requirements. This course builds literacy to commission or challenge such work; it does not qualify a learner to perform or certify an LCA.

## Lesson 8 — How environmental issues become business issues

Environmental conditions transmit into business through several routes:

- physical interruption or asset damage;
- resource availability and price;
- changing technology, market or policy conditions;
- liability, remediation and compliance cost;
- workforce/community effects;
- customer, investor or partner expectations;
- innovation, efficiency, repair, reuse and resilience opportunities.

Materiality depends on the business model. Plastic use is central for a packaging producer and minor for some service organizations. Water is critical for some processes and less so for others. A long list of global environmental topics is less useful than a disciplined map of relevant dependencies, impacts and transmission paths.

## What this means in Air Power Services

### AIR POWER SERVICES LEARNING SCENARIO — Repair, remanufacture or replace

A team evaluates three technically feasible options for an aging support component. Continued repair preserves material but requires repeated transport and energy. Remanufacture extends life and recovers high-value parts but needs a new controlled process. Replacement uses a lighter design with potential in-service efficiency but adds virgin material demand and uncertain end-of-life recovery.

The team does not award a single “green score.” It defines the function, safety and readiness constraints; compares lifecycle stages; records energy and material assumptions; identifies water, waste and supplier dependencies; and tests sensitivity to uncertain data. The recommendation can include conditions such as evidence collection, recovery arrangements or periodic review.

## Myth vs reality

### Myth 1: “Scope 3 means emissions outside our responsibility.”

**Reality:** Scope 3 describes value-chain position, not irrelevance. Influence and data quality vary, but these emissions may be material to procurement, design or service decisions.

### Myth 2: “Recycling makes a product circular.”

**Reality:** Recycling is one loop. Durability, repair, reuse and remanufacture may preserve more value. The best feasible option depends on function, safety, logistics, energy and recovery quality.

### Myth 3: “Renewable means impact-free.”

**Reality:** Renewable energy can reduce particular impacts while still requiring land, materials, infrastructure and controls. Compare systems, not labels.

## Decision lens — Environmental decision questions

1. What function and non-negotiable constraints define the comparison?
2. What lifecycle boundary and time horizon are relevant?
3. Which environmental systems does the option depend on and affect?
4. Where are the largest plausible impacts: energy, water, materials, pollution, nature or waste?
5. Which pathways could affect cost, schedule, safety, readiness or supply resilience?
6. What is measured, estimated or unknown? Are options methodologically comparable?
7. Could an improvement shift burden to another place, group or lifecycle stage?
8. What control, owner and review trigger will test whether the expected outcome occurs?

## Module close

Environmental performance is not a collection of isolated “green” actions. It is the management of dependencies, impacts and uncertainty across climate, resources and living systems.

### Key takeaways

- Climate hazards become risks through exposure and vulnerability.
- Emissions totals depend on boundaries, data and methods.
- Dependencies and impacts often run in both directions.
- Nature and resource risks are frequently location- and value-chain-specific.
- Circularity requires lifecycle reasoning and cannot override safety or readiness.
