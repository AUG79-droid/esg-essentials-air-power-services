# Module 05 — Social Risk in Global Supply Chains

## Module opening

### The dilemma

An urgent requirement can be met only if a new supplier is approved quickly. The tier-one company has passed an audit, but one subcontractor uses a labor recruiter, overtime records do not match payroll and the origin of a specialist mineral is described only as “approved source.” None of this proves that people are being harmed. None of it proves that the supply chain is safe either. What should a responsible decision-maker conclude—and what should they do next?

Global supply chains distribute technical capability, employment and economic value. They also distribute power, pressure and visibility. A buyer may be several contractual steps away from workers who experience the consequences of price, timing or design decisions. This module explains how to investigate social risk without treating suspicion as proof, and how to prioritize the risk to people as well as risk to the organization.

**Expected learning time:** 54 minutes

### Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

- distinguish stakeholders from rights-holders and risk to people from risk to the company;
- recognize source-supported indicators of severe labor and human-rights risk;
- explain how supply-chain complexity and purchasing practices can increase vulnerability;
- structure risk identification, prioritization, prevention, mitigation, monitoring and escalation;
- evaluate social audits, documents and worker voice as complementary evidence;
- explain grievance, non-retaliation, remedy and responsible disengagement at a conceptual level;
- avoid turning incomplete evidence into either proof of harm or proof of safety.

## Lesson 1 — Human rights in a business context

Human rights begin with the dignity and equal worth of people. In business, they matter because decisions about recruitment, work, purchasing, security, land, resources, products and services can affect people’s ability to live and work safely and freely. A company may affect people directly through its own employment practices, contribute through commercial pressure or be linked through products, services and business relationships.

A **stakeholder** is someone who can affect or be affected by a decision. A **rights-holder** is a person or group whose recognized rights may be affected. The distinction matters because severe harm to a rights-holder cannot be reduced to one preference in a stakeholder survey or balanced away through a high aggregate project score.

The supplied material highlights forced labor, trafficking, debt bondage, recruitment fees, unsafe conditions and vulnerable migrant workers. It also shows how complex subcontracting can conceal the people who produce goods or deliver services. ILO Convention No. 29 defines forced or compulsory labor through work or service, menace of a penalty and absence of voluntary offer; the ILO’s 2025 revised indicators and 2026 practitioner guidance support identification but are clues, not an automatic legal finding. UN and OECD frameworks call for risk-based due diligence across operations and business relationships. Applicable due-diligence, forced-labor, modern-slavery, employment and import-control law must still be mapped by entity, product and jurisdiction.

Human-rights due diligence is an ongoing management process, not a one-time audit and not a guarantee that harm will never occur. It should cover impacts the organization may cause, contribute to or be directly linked to through products, services or business relationships. It is informed by meaningful engagement with affected and potentially affected rights-holders. The method in Lesson 6 aligns the course scaffold with UNGP and OECD guidance without inventing legal duties or an APS corporate procedure.

## Lesson 2 — Risk to people and risk to the company

ESG discussions often ask how an issue could affect cost, schedule, reputation, compliance or continuity. That is **risk to the company**. Human-rights due diligence begins with a different question: how severe is the potential harm to people, and how likely is it to occur? That is **risk to people**.

The two can connect. Recruitment debt can trap workers, which may also produce legal, operational and reputational consequences for the buyer. Unsafe work can injure people and disrupt supply. Community displacement can cause severe rights impacts and later create conflict or delay. But the relationship is not guaranteed. A severe impact on a small or politically weak group may create little immediate financial effect. It still requires attention.

Prioritization therefore needs two lenses. The enterprise lens examines objectives and exposure. The people lens examines scale, scope and whether harm is difficult or impossible to remedy. Where severe harm is plausible, lack of short-term financial materiality is not a reason to ignore it.

## Lesson 3 — Severe social risk and vulnerability

Forced labor involves work extracted under threat or penalty and not offered voluntarily. Modern slavery is an umbrella term used in many contexts for severe exploitation, including forced labor and trafficking. Debt bondage can occur when debt is manipulated so a person cannot realistically leave. Recruitment fees can create or deepen debt, particularly for migrant workers who borrow money to obtain a job.

Indicators are not verdicts. Retained identity documents, restricted movement, unexplained deductions, recruitment debt, threats, excessive involuntary overtime, inconsistent payroll, isolated accommodation or inability to speak privately may raise concern. Each requires safe, competent investigation. A single indicator can have another explanation; several connected indicators may increase concern.

Vulnerability is shaped by circumstances and power. Migrant status, insecure contracts, language barriers, dependence on employer-provided housing, discrimination, conflict, poverty or lack of trusted grievance routes can reduce a worker’s ability to refuse, leave or complain. Vulnerability is not a personal defect. It is a condition created or intensified by systems.

Working conditions also matter before they reach the threshold of severe abuse. Hours, pay, safety, harassment, discrimination, freedom of association, accommodation and access to remedy affect people and business continuity. Legal thresholds and definitions vary by jurisdiction and must be confirmed from applicable law and collective agreements rather than inferred from the source transcript.

## Lesson 4 — Why risk hides across tiers

A tier-one supplier may assemble a component while raw materials, surface treatments, electronics, packaging and labor services come from several other companies. Authorized and unauthorized subcontracting can occur. Labor recruiters may sit outside the product contract. Audits may cover a facility but not dormitories, labor agencies or home-based work.

Visibility is not the same as control. A buyer may know supplier names but not understand processes, locations or workforce conditions. Conversely, incomplete mapping does not prove abuse. The purpose of mapping is to identify where severe harm could occur and where evidence is weakest.

Risk is influenced by geography, sector, process, workforce and commercial conditions. A high-risk context is not proof that every supplier in it is harmful; a low-risk location is not proof of safety. Prioritization should combine context with relationship-specific evidence.

Mineral extraction can connect environmental and social risk. Land disturbance, water contamination or resource competition may affect livelihoods and communities. Extraction and processing may also involve dangerous work or opaque intermediaries. The environmental and social pillars meet through the same value chain.

## Lesson 5 — Purchasing practices can cause risk

Social risk is not located only “at the supplier.” Buyer behavior can contribute. Unrealistically low prices, compressed lead times, volatile forecasts, late engineering changes, delayed payment and sudden volume swings can create pressure for excessive hours, informal labor or unauthorized subcontracting.

This does not excuse supplier misconduct. It improves causal analysis. A corrective action that tells a supplier to reduce overtime while the buyer continues to demand impossible delivery can move records rather than reality. Prevention may require changes to planning, contracting, forecasting, escalation or design—not only another audit.

Leverage is the ability to influence behavior. It can arise from spend, long-term relationship, technical support, contract terms, collaboration with other buyers or access to capacity-building. Low leverage does not end responsibility to understand risk; it changes the available response and may make collective action or escalation more important.

### Test the buyer’s contribution

For a material supplier risk, ask five questions before demanding another supplier corrective action:

1. **Requirement:** Were specifications, quality expectations and evidence requirements clear and stable?
2. **Time:** Could a capable supplier meet the lead time without excessive hours, unsafe shortcuts or unauthorized subcontracting?
3. **Volume and forecast:** Did unstable orders, cancellations or emergency demand create avoidable workforce pressure?
4. **Price and payment:** Does the commercial model allow lawful wages, safe work and required controls, and are payments timely?
5. **Change and leverage:** Did late buyer changes create the problem, and can technical support, planning, contract terms, capacity-building or collective action improve it?

Record both supplier actions and buyer actions. Responsible contracting translates relevant expectations into clear terms, but a clause is not due diligence: the buyer must share usable information, provide realistic implementation conditions, monitor outcomes and avoid incentives that contradict the requirement.

## Lesson 6 — A conceptual due-diligence cycle

The source provides strong risk and audit insights but not a complete due-diligence method. The following sequence is therefore a learning scaffold, not a legal statement:

1. **Map and understand:** Identify products, services, tiers, locations, processes, workforce models and affected communities.
2. **Identify indicators:** Use context, incidents, complaints, audits, records and stakeholder insight.
3. **Prioritize potential harm:** Give greatest attention to severity for people, then consider likelihood and enterprise exposure.
4. **Prevent:** Change requirements, purchasing practices, recruitment arrangements or controls before harm occurs.
5. **Mitigate:** Reduce the likelihood or severity of an impact that cannot immediately be prevented.
6. **Escalate:** Bring severe, systemic, conflicted or unresolved issues to the authority capable of acting.
7. **Track:** Examine whether actions occur and whether conditions change.
8. **Communicate:** Provide decision-relevant information without exposing workers or making unsupported claims.
9. **Enable remedy:** Where harm occurred, address the consequence for affected people, not only the procedural failure.

This sequence aligns with the OECD cycle and UN Guiding Principles. When prioritisation is necessary, assess negative human-rights impacts primarily by severity: **scale**, **scope** and **irremediable character**. Likelihood helps prioritise potential impacts, but a low-probability severe harm may still require urgent prevention. Enterprise cost or reputation can inform the business response; they must not displace severity to people.

Engage affected rights-holders safely at appropriate points: to understand impact, design prevention or remedy, and test effectiveness. Document why issues were prioritised and what would trigger reassessment. Due diligence is iterative because relationships, evidence and impacts change.

## Lesson 7 — Social audits: useful evidence, limited proof

A social audit examines selected working conditions, records and practices against criteria. Audits can provide structured coverage, identify control weaknesses and support corrective action. They can also fail.

The Matt Friedman source describes limitations: visits may be announced; records can be prepared; workers may be coached; interview samples may be unrepresentative; auditors may face commercial conflicts; and a short visit captures a moment rather than continuous reality. A passed audit means the audit did not identify certain non-conformities within its scope and method. It does not prove that no abuse exists.

Document evidence is necessary but can diverge from lived reality. Payroll may not match hours worked. Policies may not be understood. A grievance channel may exist but be distrusted. Conversely, a record inconsistency is not itself proof of forced labor. Investigation should triangulate documents, observation and protected worker voice.

Interview safeguards are essential. Workers must be able to speak privately, understand the purpose, avoid retaliation and know how information will be used. Poor interviewing can endanger the people it is meant to protect. At awareness level, check that participation is voluntary; the setting is private and safe; management or recruiters cannot select or observe participants; the interviewer is competent and sufficiently independent; language and power imbalances are addressed; confidentiality and its limits are explained; questions do not expose unnecessary identity; records are secure; and follow-up and retaliation monitoring are defined. Never promise absolute confidentiality or a remedy the process cannot deliver. The audit owner must select a current protocol suitable for the issue and jurisdiction. Specialist investigators handle severe allegations; this course does not qualify learners to interview victims or witnesses.

## Lesson 8 — Worker voice, grievance and non-retaliation

A grievance mechanism allows people to raise concerns and seek response. Its quality depends on awareness, accessibility, trust, confidentiality, timeliness, fairness, protection and remedy. A hotline number on a poster is an input, not proof that workers can use it safely.

Low reporting has several explanations: low harm, lack of awareness, language barriers, distrust, fear, inaccessible technology or previous failure to act. High reporting can reflect deteriorating conditions or growing trust. Interpretation requires context and trend.

Non-retaliation is central because workers who depend on income, accommodation or immigration status may face serious consequences for speaking. The organization must consider indirect retaliation by supervisors, recruiters or peers. Channel design, confidentiality, personal-data handling, investigation and protection measures must be checked against applicable whistleblowing, grievance, privacy and worker-protection law.

Test effectiveness as a loop: **access → safe intake → assessment and escalation → response → remedy where appropriate → feedback closure → learning**. The UNGP effectiveness criteria ask whether a non-judicial mechanism is legitimate, accessible, predictable, equitable, transparent, rights-compatible, a source of learning and—at operational level—based on engagement and dialogue. Record cases securely and analyse patterns without exposing users. Case closure means the agreed process ended; it does not prove that the concern was resolved, retaliation was prevented or remedy was effective.

## Lesson 9 — Remedy and responsible disengagement

Stopping a business relationship can reduce the buyer’s exposure while worsening harm to workers through sudden job loss, unpaid wages or loss of leverage. Continuing without conditions can also sustain harm. Responsible disengagement considers severity, ability to influence, the supplier’s response, consequences for people and whether exit can be staged or accompanied by remedy.

Remedy addresses the impact experienced by people. Correcting a policy or closing an audit finding may prevent recurrence but does not necessarily restore unpaid wages, documents, health, freedom or livelihood. The appropriate response depends on the harm and the organization’s connection to it.

Keep four responses distinct: **stop ongoing harm**, **prevent recurrence**, **correct the failed process**, and **remedy effects on people**. One action may support several aims, but none should be assumed. Remedy can include acknowledgement, restitution, rehabilitation, financial or non-financial compensation, sanctions or guarantees of non-repetition depending on context and lawful authority. Do not invent a remedy without affected-person input and specialist advice. A management action that improves a procedure while leaving affected people with the loss is corrective action, not complete remedy.

Evidence gaps must be handled carefully. “We could not verify harm” is different from “no harm exists.” “A risk indicator is present” is different from “the supplier committed abuse.” Responsible decisions preserve these distinctions in documentation and communication.

## Lesson 10 — Communities, land and resource-related impacts

Supply-chain social risk is not limited to employment. Mining, processing, logistics and facilities can affect community water, land access, health, safety and livelihoods. An environmental change can become a social impact through the people who depend on the affected system.

Community evidence has its own challenges. A local authority, civil-society organization, landowner or community leader may hold valuable information, but no single representative necessarily speaks for everyone. Groups within a community can experience an activity differently, and people with less formal power may be least visible in standard consultation.

Decision-makers should distinguish an allegation, a documented environmental change, a demonstrated livelihood effect and a verified rights impact. These can form a causal chain, but one does not automatically prove the next. Location, timing, cumulative effects and access to remedy matter.

Legal authorization is important but does not by itself establish engagement quality or fair distribution of consequences. Applicable land, Indigenous Peoples’ rights, environmental-justice and community-consultation requirements depend on location, rights-holders, project and jurisdiction and require specialist applicability review.

For an initial community-impact screen:

1. identify affected and potentially affected groups, including less visible or vulnerable groups;
2. map pathways from the activity to water, land, health, safety, access, culture, livelihood or services;
3. consider baseline vulnerability and unequal distribution of benefits and burdens;
4. examine cumulative effects alongside other activities and changes;
5. gather local and technical evidence, recording uncertainty and disagreement;
6. avoid or mitigate impacts and define owners, resources and escalation;
7. create accessible feedback or grievance routes and explain responses;
8. monitor actual effects with affected people and adapt action.

Meaningful engagement is two-way and early enough to influence a decision. Sending a notice after key choices have been fixed is communication, not consultation. This screen does not replace a formal environmental/social impact assessment, human-rights assessment or legally required consultation.

## Lesson 11 — Monitoring effectiveness rather than closure

Corrective actions often focus on artifacts: a policy issued, workers trained, recruitment contracts changed or a finding marked closed. These show activity. Effectiveness asks whether workers stopped paying fees, retained documents, received repayment, experienced safer hours and could raise concerns without retaliation.

Evidence may include reconciled records, repeat protected interviews, grievance trends, observation and confirmation that remedy reached affected people. Follow-up timing matters: rapid closure may confirm a document change but miss whether practice endured.

Monitoring should also look for displacement. A supplier can correct one site while moving production to an unassessed sub-tier. Overtime can disappear from official records while informal work grows. A credible programme watches the causal pathway, not only the original indicator.

Effectiveness evidence should be interpreted with affected people, not only about them. A management report may show fee repayment while workers describe new deductions under another name. A grievance dashboard may show faster closure while complainants experience no change. This does not make management data useless; it shows why lived experience and operational records should be compared.

Monitoring also needs a stopping rule. Repeated promises, missed corrective actions or retaliation can show that engagement is not reducing harm. The governance process should define when conditions move from improvement support to stronger escalation, suspension or responsible disengagement. Conversely, the process should recognize sustained improvement so suppliers are not trapped by historical findings after controls and remedy have become effective.

The final question is not “Did we complete due diligence?” Due diligence is ongoing. The better question is “What changed for the people at risk, what evidence supports that conclusion, and what could make us revise it?”

That question keeps the process connected to actual conditions instead of allowing completed forms, supplier promises or favorable averages to become substitutes for human outcomes.

## What this means in Air Power Services

### AIR POWER SERVICES LEARNING SCENARIO — Constrained electronics supply

An urgent retrofit needs a specialist electronic module. The proposed tier-one supplier has technical capability and a recent audit. Evidence shows a new subcontractor, labor-recruiter fees described as “worker service charges,” inconsistent overtime totals and mineral origin aggregated by region. The module is not yet approved for use, and safety, airworthiness, security and technical-authority requirements remain mandatory.

The team separates technical approval from social-risk investigation. It does not infer forced labor from one inconsistency, and it does not infer safety from the passed audit. It requests recruiter contracts, fee records, payroll/time reconciliation, private worker-interview safeguards, sub-tier mapping and mineral traceability scope. It also examines whether its urgent lead time is creating pressure. Severe indicators are escalated through the appropriate governance route.

## Myth vs reality

### Myth 1: “A passed audit proves there is no forced labor.”

**Reality:** An audit supports a conclusion only within its scope, timing, method and evidence. Severe harm can be hidden; an audit finding can also be misunderstood without investigation.

### Myth 2: “No evidence of harm means evidence of no harm.”

**Reality:** Missing evidence creates uncertainty. The response depends on severity indicators, evidence access and decision consequences.

### Myth 3: “Social risk belongs to the supplier.”

**Reality:** Supplier controls matter, but buyer price, timing, forecasts and changes can contribute to risk.

### Myth 4: “Immediate termination is always the most ethical response.”

**Reality:** Exit can protect or harm people. Decisions should consider severity, leverage, remedy and the consequence of withdrawal.

## Decision lens

1. Who are the rights-holders, and what severe harm could occur?
2. What is risk to people? Separately, what is risk to the organization?
3. Which tiers, processes, labor intermediaries and communities are in scope?
4. What is known, unknown, an indicator, an allegation or verified evidence?
5. Could our price, schedule, forecast or change behavior be contributing?
6. Do audits and documents align with observation and protected worker voice?
7. What requires immediate protection or escalation?
8. What prevention, mitigation, monitoring or remedy is proportionate?
9. Would disengagement reduce or worsen harm, and how should the decision be documented?

## Module close

Supply-chain social risk is not solved by collecting certificates. It requires visibility, careful evidence, attention to power and a response centered on people as well as enterprise risk.

### Key takeaways

- Rights-holder risk and enterprise risk overlap but are not interchangeable.
- Indicators require investigation; they are neither proof nor permission to ignore concern.
- Purchasing practices can contribute to labor risk.
- Audits are one evidence source and must be triangulated with records, observation and safe worker voice.
- Remedy addresses effects on people; responsible disengagement considers consequences of exit.
