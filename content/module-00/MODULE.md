# Module 00 — ESG: Why It Exists

## Module opening

### The dilemma

A supplier offers a component that meets the technical specification, arrives on time and reduces near-term cost. Yet the material provenance is unclear, the supplier’s workforce data is inconsistent and the commercial team cannot explain who approved the evidence. Is this an environmental issue, a social issue, a governance issue—or simply a procurement issue?

The useful answer is not “pick one.” Real business decisions often create several kinds of effect at once. ESG exists because conventional decision information can miss environmental dependencies, effects on people and weaknesses in the way decisions are controlled. It gives decision-makers a disciplined way to ask what else could affect the organization’s ability to create durable value—and what consequences the organization may create for others.

This module establishes the vocabulary and logic used throughout the course. It does not ask you to adopt a slogan. It asks you to widen the evidence considered before a decision is made.

**Expected learning time:** 35 minutes

### Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

- distinguish sustainability from ESG;
- explain Environmental, Social and Governance as connected dimensions;
- identify stakeholders and externalities at a beginner level;
- connect ESG issues to risk, opportunity and long-term value;
- explain why governance can prevent—or amplify—organizational failure;
- use ESG as a decision framework rather than a marketing label.

## Lesson 1 — Sustainability and ESG are related, but not identical

Sustainability is the broad idea that an activity, organization or system should be capable of continuing without undermining the environmental and social conditions on which it depends. It asks whether choices made today preserve the ability of people, organizations and wider systems to function in the future. In business, this includes continuity, resilience and the quality of relationships that make operations possible.

ESG is a practical lens for examining a part of that broad challenge. The letters point to environmental, social and governance information that may be relevant to decisions, oversight and accountability. ESG does not replace strategy, finance, engineering, safety, quality, ethics or compliance. It changes the questions those disciplines ask and the evidence they consider.

The distinction matters. A company can publish ESG data without having a sustainable business model. It can improve one environmental metric while shifting harm to workers, suppliers or another stage of the lifecycle. Equally, a decision can contribute to sustainability even if it is not labeled “ESG.” The label is less important than the quality of the reasoning and evidence.

Environmental information concerns how an organization depends on and affects climate, energy, water, land, materials, air, waste and living systems. Social information concerns how decisions affect people: employees, contractors, supply-chain workers, customers, communities and others. Governance concerns how the organization is directed and controlled: who decides, who challenges, what evidence is used, how conflicts are managed and what happens when something goes wrong.

These are not three unrelated lists. A mineral-sourcing decision may affect land and water, working conditions, community rights, delivery resilience and the integrity of supplier approval. The pillars help organize inquiry; they should not break a connected system into isolated boxes.

## Lesson 2 — Why ordinary business information can be incomplete

Organizations routinely monitor cost, schedule, technical performance and compliance. Those measures are necessary, but they may not show the full consequences of a decision. A low purchase price may depend on resource depletion, unsafe work or a supplier that is concealing subcontracting. A high-efficiency asset may require difficult-to-source materials or become hard to repair. A target may look impressive while excluding the most significant part of the value chain.

An **externality** is a consequence of an activity that is not fully reflected in the transaction between buyer and seller. Pollution imposed on a community is a common negative example. Workforce skills created through training can generate positive effects beyond the immediate transaction. “External” does not mean irrelevant. Consequences can return to the organization through regulation, litigation, resource scarcity, disrupted supply, employee response, customer expectations, investor decisions or loss of trust.

ESG therefore asks decision-makers to examine more than the immediate transaction. What does the organization rely on? What does it change? Who experiences the consequences? Which consequences could return as business risk or opportunity? Which effects matter even before they are translated into money?

This is not a promise that every effect can be measured precisely. Some environmental quantities can be counted; some social experiences require qualitative evidence; governance quality can be partly observed through structures and partly through behavior. The correct response to uncertainty is not to ignore the issue or pretend precision. It is to state the uncertainty, collect proportionate evidence and make the decision traceable.

## Lesson 3 — Stakeholders and affected people

A stakeholder is a person or group that can affect, be affected by or reasonably care about an organization’s decisions. Shareholders and customers are stakeholders, but so are employees, contractors, suppliers, regulators, local communities and civil-society organizations. Some stakeholders hold specific rights. Calling someone a stakeholder must not dilute obligations owed to them as a worker, rights-holder or affected community.

Stakeholder thinking improves decision quality when it reveals information that the decision team does not hold. Maintenance professionals may see practical consequences that a project plan misses. Supply-chain workers may identify recruitment fees or unpaid overtime that an audit does not reveal. A local community may understand water availability or transport impacts that are invisible in a corporate dataset.

Stakeholder attention does not mean that every preference receives equal weight or that technical authorities surrender responsibility. It means decision-makers identify who is affected, understand relevant evidence and explain trade-offs. Safety, legality and human rights cannot be treated as popularity contests.

## Lesson 4 — From impact and dependency to risk and opportunity

Four concepts help connect ESG to business decisions.

An **impact** is a change the organization causes or contributes to in the environment or society. A **dependency** is something the organization relies on, such as stable energy, water, skilled labor, trustworthy suppliers or effective institutions. A **risk** is uncertainty that could affect objectives. An **opportunity** is a condition or action that could improve performance, resilience or positive outcomes.

The sequence often works in both directions. A facility depends on reliable water. Regional scarcity may create operating disruption and cost. The facility may also affect local availability through its own use. Reducing consumption can therefore reduce impact, lower exposure and improve resilience. But the connection is not automatic: an intervention may require capital, create another lifecycle burden or produce only marginal change.

ESG information becomes useful when it changes a decision. It can alter requirements, supplier selection, project timing, monitoring, control design, contingency planning or investment. Collecting data without linking it to a decision may create reporting activity without better performance.

## Lesson 5 — Long-term value and the role of governance

Long-term value is not simply a distant financial result. It depends on whether an organization can keep performing while maintaining the resources, relationships, capabilities and legitimacy it needs. Short-term performance can conceal weakening controls, depleted assets, loss of expertise or growing stakeholder harm.

Governance is the system of rules, roles, practices and processes by which an organization is directed and controlled. It determines who has authority, what must be reported, how leaders are challenged, how incentives are set and how conduct is investigated. Good governance does not guarantee a good outcome, but weak governance makes it easier for poor information, unmanaged conflicts and excessive pressure to persist.

Governance also connects the E and S pillars to action. An emissions commitment needs a defined boundary, accountable owner, resources, measures, controls and oversight. A human-rights policy needs supplier requirements, worker-accessible channels, investigation, remedy and review. Without governance, an ESG commitment may remain a statement.

## Case perspective — Enron and a chain of governance failure

The supplied Enron case describes rapid growth supported by complex accounting, special-purpose entities used to conceal liabilities, conflicts involving the external auditor, ignored warning signs and a culture in which dissent was suppressed. The learning value is not that one technical accounting method alone caused collapse. It is the interaction among incentives, opacity, weak challenge, control failure and misleading public information.

The case also shows how consequences spread. Enron filed for Chapter 11 protection on 2 December 2001. Employees lost jobs, investors and retirement savers lost value, and trust in corporate reporting and audit was damaged. Arthur Andersen was convicted of obstruction in 2002, stopped practising before the US Securities and Exchange Commission that year, and had its conviction reversed by the US Supreme Court in 2005 because the jury instructions did not properly state the required elements. In July 2002, the United States enacted the Sarbanes–Oxley Act; among other reforms, it created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board and strengthened issuer reporting, audit oversight and auditor-independence requirements. Enron was one of several scandals that shaped the reform response. It did not “start” ESG, and any such claim is an interpretation rather than settled history.

The practical lesson is that governance is not administrative overhead added after the real work. It is part of how an organization knows whether reported performance is credible and whether bad news can reach the people who must act.

## What this means in Air Power Services

### AIR POWER SERVICES LEARNING SCENARIO — The apparently complete supplier decision

A procurement team is choosing between two suppliers for a non-classified, non-safety-critical support component. Both meet the specification. Supplier A has a higher unit price but provides traceable material records, stable workforce indicators and clear subcontractor controls. Supplier B is cheaper and faster but supplies only a sustainability badge, a two-page audit summary and an assurance that all sub-tier suppliers are “approved.”

An ESG lens does not dictate “choose A.” It changes the work required before deciding. The team asks which material and labor risks are relevant, whether evidence reaches beyond tier one, who validated it, how schedule pressure may influence supplier behavior and what contractual or monitoring controls would reduce uncertainty. It also records what is not known. Technical compliance remains essential; ESG evidence makes the commercial and operational view more complete.

## Myth vs reality

### Myth 1: “ESG is another word for environmental sustainability.”

**Reality:** Environmental information is one pillar. People and governance are equally important, and many decisions cross all three. Treating ESG as only carbon or waste can hide labor risk, stakeholder effects and control failure.

### Myth 2: “If an activity is legal, ESG adds nothing.”

**Reality:** Compliance establishes obligations, but law may not capture every emerging risk, dependency or stakeholder effect. ESG can also reveal opportunities and help prepare for changing conditions. It must not, however, be used to invent legal duties.

### Myth 3: “A high ESG score proves a company is sustainable.”

**Reality:** A score reflects a methodology, boundary, data set and purpose. It may be useful, but it is not proof of overall performance. Module 03 examines this limitation in depth.

## Decision lens — What should I ask?

Before making or recommending a decision, ask:

1. What objective are we trying to achieve, and over what time horizon?
2. What environmental and social systems does the decision depend on or affect?
3. Who is affected, including people beyond the immediate contract or team?
4. What risks and opportunities could change cost, schedule, quality, readiness, reputation or legitimacy?
5. What evidence supports the claims, and what remains uncertain?
6. Who owns the decision, who provides independent challenge and what must be escalated?
7. Could the proposed benefit shift harm to another group, place or lifecycle stage?

## Module close

ESG exists because important consequences and dependencies can sit outside conventional decision measures. It organizes inquiry into environmental conditions, effects on people and the quality of organizational control. Its value is not the label; its value is the ability to improve decisions, expose uncertainty and assign accountability.

### Key takeaways

- Sustainability is the broad goal; ESG is a decision and accountability lens.
- Environmental, social and governance issues form a connected system.
- Externalities can return as business consequences even when absent from the initial price.
- Stakeholder evidence can reveal blind spots, but decision responsibility remains governed.
- Governance turns commitments into accountable action and helps prevent failure.
