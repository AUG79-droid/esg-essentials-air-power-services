# Air Power Services ESG Decision Lab

## Simulation design

The lab contains eight connected stages. Choices alter later evidence, time, trust and control options. The simulation must never present a numeric “green score.” Mandatory technical constraints are rendered as locked gates, not weighted criteria.

## Stage 1 — Frame the decision

### Information

Inventory supports a limited period. Repair yield is declining. The new supplier claims equivalent functionality and faster delivery, but technical qualification is incomplete. Programme management asks for “ESG approval” to strengthen the recommendation.

### Learner task

Choose the decision statement.

- Approve the new supplier as sustainable.
- Choose the option with the best aggregate KPI.
- Define a governed comparison among technically feasible pathways, with technical qualification as a mandatory gate.
- Delay all work until every ESG uncertainty is eliminated.

### Feedback

The strong choice defines what ESG can and cannot decide. “ESG approval” is rejected because ESG is not a substitute authority. Aggregate KPI selection can hide severe or mandatory issues. Waiting for perfect information may create unmanaged readiness risk.

### Downstream effect

If the learner frames correctly, later evidence is organized by decision and authority. Weak framing produces repeated prompts about scope and gate ownership.

## Stage 2 — Boundaries and affected parties

### Information

The alternative involves electronic components, a specialist mineral, a changed surface treatment, new packaging, air freight during transition and overtime at two facilities. Contractors support installation. A local community may be affected by a sub-tier processing facility.

### Learner task

Build the boundary: lifecycle stages, supplier tiers, workforce groups, rights-holders, operational dependencies and governance bodies. Select which exclusions require justification.

### Feedback

A narrow facility boundary misses material, treatment, logistics and sub-tier effects. An unlimited boundary becomes unusable. Strong reasoning identifies the most decision-relevant pathways, affected people and evidence gaps while documenting exclusions.

### Downstream effect

Mapped groups create later worker/community evidence. Omitted tiers remain unknown and reduce confidence rather than being assumed safe.

## Stage 3 — Environmental dependencies and impacts

### Information

The alternative component is lighter. The supplier reports lower operational electricity per unit. The changed treatment uses less process energy but a chemical with incomplete waste and worker-exposure data. Rapid transition requires air freight. Repair preserves material but has falling yield and repeated transport.

### Learner task

Classify dependency, impact, claim, evidence and unknown. Choose two analyses or evidence requests and identify burden-shifting risks.

### Feedback

“Lighter” is not whole-lifecycle superiority. Facility energy is a bounded metric. Changed chemical treatment links environmental and social evidence. Air freight may be transitional but still belongs in the boundary. Strong choices define function, baseline, lifecycle stages and uncertainty.

### Downstream effect

Requesting treatment/waste data reveals disposal destination and worker-control gaps. Requesting only carbon produces a narrower later option set.

## Stage 4 — Supplier and social investigation

### Information

The tier-one audit passed. A labor recruiter is used at a sub-tier; some fee deductions are unclear. Overtime records conflict with payroll. Mineral origin is regional, not facility-specific. No verified harm is established.

### Learner task

Separate known, unknown, indicator, allegation and verified harm. Select protected evidence requests, examine buyer schedule contribution and choose escalation.

### Feedback

Strong reasoning neither declares abuse nor declares safety. It prioritizes potential severity to people, protects worker interviews, requests recruiter/payroll/sub-tier evidence and recognizes that compressed lead time may contribute. Technical qualification continues separately.

### Downstream effect

Protected inquiry can reveal fees or explain discrepancies. Unsafe interview selection reduces evidence reliability and creates a safeguarding warning. Abrupt exit may remove the supplier but worsens worker-impact uncertainty.

## Stage 5 — Claims, data and uncertainty

### Information

Marketing proposes “lower-carbon and responsibly sourced retrofit.” The evidence includes a supplier certificate, modeled energy reduction, incomplete mineral mapping and no operational outcome because the component is not yet in service.

### Learner task

Sort `CLAIM`, `EVIDENCE`, `METRIC`, `TARGET`, `OUTCOME` and `UNKNOWN`. Choose a supportable internal statement and decide what may be communicated externally.

### Feedback

The strong statement is narrow and conditional: modeled facility/process energy within a defined boundary, pending qualification and evidence. “Responsibly sourced” is unsupported. No outcome exists yet. A learner who rejects all information is reminded that bounded evidence can still support internal comparison.

### Downstream effect

Overclaiming raises reputation/control exposure in later governance review. Calibrated language improves trust.

## Stage 6 — Governance and escalation

### Information

Programme schedule is red. Technical authority has not approved equivalence. Procurement owns the supplier relationship, quality verified specified records, compliance is reviewing social indicators and the environmental model has no independent check. The dashboard shows “supplier approved” because commercial onboarding is complete.

### Learner task

Map objective, risk, control, evidence, monitoring, authority, escalation and corrective action. Correct the dashboard. Decide what can remain operational and what requires senior or technical escalation.

### Feedback

Strong governance separates commercial onboarding, technical approval and ESG evidence. It corrects misleading status, assigns owners, escalates severe/uncertain matters and protects mandatory gates. Sending everything to the board is not strong; neither is leaving cross-functional material uncertainty below authority thresholds.

### Downstream effect

Correct escalation unlocks resources for a controlled interim option. Delayed escalation reduces schedule flexibility.

## Stage 7 — Compare options

### Information

The learner now sees an option matrix:

- continued repair: known technical path, declining yield, material preservation, repeated logistics, limited duration;
- incumbent redesign: longer schedule, stronger relationship evidence, new qualification and cost;
- new supplier: shorter stated lead time, lower cost, incomplete technical/social/environmental evidence;
- controlled interim: staged availability, additional governance and cost, time to close evidence.

### Learner task

Identify non-compensable constraints, compare lifecycle and people/company risks, choose option or conditional sequence, and state residual uncertainty.

### Feedback

No option dominates. Strong reasoning may choose a staged combination, but only if technical authority and monitoring are explicit. A low price cannot offset mandatory failure or severe rights harm. A long schedule cannot be ignored simply because evidence quality is higher.

### Downstream effect

Conditions selected here become the monitoring plan.

## Stage 8 — Document and monitor

### Learner task

Produce the decision record:

- objective and authority;
- options considered and counterfactual;
- evidence and sources;
- affected parties and pillar interactions;
- mandatory constraints;
- assumptions and residual uncertainty;
- decision and conditions;
- owners, indicators and thresholds;
- escalation and corrective-action route;
- review date and triggers.

### Feedback dimensions

**STRONG:** Evidence is bounded and triangulated; severe people-risk and mandatory gates are protected; cross-pillar trade-offs are explicit; ownership and monitoring are decision-linked.  
**DEVELOPING:** Main risks are recognized but evidence categories, authority, lifecycle boundary or monitoring are incomplete.  
**NEEDS REVIEW:** Labels or aggregate scores substitute for evidence; technical authority is bypassed; severe rights indicators are ignored or asserted as proven; uncertainty is concealed; no traceable conditions exist.

## Substantial debrief

### What strong learners usually do well

They frame the decision before choosing a metric. They keep technical approval separate and mandatory. They distinguish risk to people from risk to company, and they avoid converting evidence gaps into verdicts. They examine environmental burden shifting, buyer contribution, controls and downstream monitoring. Their final recommendation is conditional where evidence is conditional.

### Evidence commonly overlooked

- the functional/lifecycle baseline behind “lighter” or “lower energy”;
- chemical treatment, waste destination and worker exposure;
- recruiter contracts and whether fees are repaid;
- unauthorized or unmapped sub-tiers;
- the difference between commercial onboarding and technical approval;
- who checked the environmental model;
- worker and contractor voice during schedule pressure;
- the effect of buyer forecasting and change requests;
- the counterfactual if no option is approved.

### Pillar interactions

The alternative material is environmental and social because extraction and processing affect ecosystems, communities and workers. The schedule is operational and social because pressure affects working time and subcontracting. The claim is environmental and governance because its credibility depends on boundary, evidence, review and authority. Readiness is a business objective governed through mandatory technical controls.

### Trade-offs and unintended consequences

Continuing repair can preserve material while increasing repeated processing and threatening future availability. Accelerating a supplier can protect schedule while creating pressure or bypass risk. Abrupt disengagement can reduce buyer exposure while harming workers. A staged option can create time but requires cost, control and monitoring. Seemingly reasonable decisions create unintended effects when the system boundary is too narrow.

### What must be monitored

Technical qualification status; component performance; repair yield; material and sub-tier traceability; recruitment-fee remediation; working-time and grievance evidence; treatment chemical and waste controls; logistics mode; modeled versus actual energy; supplier corrective actions; control exceptions; and whether decision conditions remain valid.

### What remains uncertain

Not every sub-tier or lifecycle effect can be known at approval. Models depend on use and data assumptions. Worker evidence may remain incomplete. Future demand and repair yield may change. The decision record must identify which uncertainty is accepted, which is temporary, and which triggers pause or escalation.

### Why reasonable teams can disagree

Two careful teams may prefer different conditional pathways because they assign different probability to repair yield, supplier readiness or evidence closure. Disagreement is not automatically a governance defect. The quality test is whether each team uses the same mandatory constraints, represents evidence honestly, considers affected people and explains what would change its recommendation.

Disagreement becomes risky when assumptions remain implicit, one function’s authority is treated as universal approval, or schedule pressure prevents challenge. A transparent decision may record a minority view and still proceed. The monitoring plan should pay particular attention to the assumptions on which the disagreement turned.

### How to respond when conditions deteriorate

Suppose the selected option passes the technical gate but a sub-tier worker later reports recruitment debt, or the waste contractor cannot confirm final treatment. The correct response is not to defend the original decision because it was reasonable at the time. New evidence changes the decision context.

The team should protect affected people where relevant, preserve evidence, reassess severity and connection, activate escalation thresholds and determine whether containment, investigation, corrective action, remedy or pause is required. It should also ask why monitoring detected the issue when it did and whether the original control was adequately designed.

Conversely, improved evidence can reduce uncertainty. Verified fee repayment, protected worker confirmation, stable control operation and traceable waste treatment can support continued engagement. Governance should allow confidence to change in both directions.

### Transfer to everyday work

Most learners will not make the exact decision in this simulation. The transferable practice is smaller and more frequent:

- clarify what is being approved;
- identify what the evidence actually establishes;
- keep mandatory authority visible;
- ask who is affected beyond the immediate process;
- look for lifecycle and value-chain displacement;
- separate severe impact from aggregate scoring;
- record uncertainty and conditions;
- monitor whether assumptions become outcomes.

An engineer may apply this when comparing materials. A maintenance professional may use it when deciding repair or replacement. Procurement may apply it to supplier evidence and commercial pressure. Project managers may integrate thresholds into gates. Quality and compliance professionals may clarify assurance scope. Managers may protect dissent and resource corrective action.

### Educational completion

Completing the lab demonstrates engagement with the course reasoning, not authorization to approve a technical change, determine legal compliance, conduct a human-rights investigation or speak for the company. Real decisions require the relevant policies, competent specialists and delegated authorities. The course gives a common language for bringing better questions and evidence into those processes.

### Central course idea

ESG is not a label attached after the decision. It is part of how evidence, impacts, risks, people and governance are considered before and during the decision.
