# Module 08 — ESG into Business Strategy

## Module opening

“Improve responsible sourcing” sounds positive. It does not define which harm or opportunity matters, which suppliers or materials are in scope, who owns action, what evidence changes a decision or how success will be recognized. How does a theme become strategy?

Implementation means integrating material ESG information into ordinary business choices: strategy, requirements, investment, design, procurement, projects, operations, people processes, risk and review. It should not create a parallel bureaucracy disconnected from how work is governed.

**Expected learning time:** 44 minutes

### Learning objectives

You will translate ESG themes into priorities and objectives; use stakeholder insight and materiality cautiously; distinguish activity, performance, metric, target, achievement, reporting and impact; design actions, indicators, ownership and review; integrate ESG into project lifecycles; and manage change, uncertainty and learning.

## Lesson 1 — From topic list to material priority

An organization could list hundreds of environmental, social and governance topics. Strategy requires choices. A materiality process examines context, value chain, affected people, dependencies, impacts, risks, opportunities and time horizons to identify what warrants management attention.

Financial materiality asks what sustainability-related risks or opportunities could affect the organization’s prospects and information users’ decisions. Impact materiality asks how the organization has or could have significant effects on people and environment. Double materiality considers both directions and their connections. An impact can become a financial risk, but it can be impact-material before that happens; an issue can also be financially material without being a significant impact caused by the organization.

Use the materiality process introduced in Module 02:

1. define decision/reporting purpose, scope, users and time horizons;
2. understand activities, business relationships, locations and value chain;
3. identify actual and potential impacts, dependencies, risks and opportunities;
4. gather operational, scientific, financial and rights-holder/stakeholder evidence;
5. assess impact significance using severity—scale, scope and irremediable character—and likelihood for potential impacts;
6. separately assess possible financial magnitude, timing and likelihood using appropriate qualitative and quantitative evidence;
7. apply documented thresholds and judgment, validate with governance, experts and relevant affected people or information users;
8. record inclusions, exclusions, uncertainty, dissent and review triggers.

Do not mechanically multiply uncertain scores or offset a severe impact with an opportunity. A matrix is a decision aid, not the decision. The issue is material under a double-materiality approach if it meets the applicable impact or financial threshold; it need not meet both.

In the EU reporting context, ESRS uses double materiality, but applicability depends on CSRD scope, period and legally effective standards. EFRAG IG 1 illustrates a process but is non-authoritative implementation guidance. ISSB uses an investor-focused perspective and should not be relabelled as double materiality. This beginner method supports strategy even outside reporting; it does not establish ESRS compliance.

**EXPLORE FURTHER — Detailed materiality assessment:** a formal reporting assessment requires the complete applicable standards, value-chain analysis, documented thresholds, disclosure mapping and current governance/assurance requirements. Use qualified reporting specialists.

Prioritization is not a popularity vote. Stakeholder insight can reveal blind spots, but technical evidence, rights severity and strategic exposure also matter. The process should record why an issue is included, excluded or deferred and what would trigger reassessment.

## Lesson 2 — Define the issue before the ambition

Broad ambition should be converted into a defined problem. “Responsible sourcing” could concern forced labor in mineral extraction, material provenance, environmental impact, supply resilience, corruption or all of these. Each implies different evidence and action.

A useful issue definition includes scope, affected systems and people, baseline condition, causal pathways, uncertainty and decision context. It states constraints. For Air Power Services, safety, airworthiness, security and technical authority remain mandatory; ESG information helps choose among feasible options and manage their consequences.

## Lesson 3 — Objective, action and ownership

An objective describes a result the organization seeks. An action describes what it will do. “Conduct 100 supplier audits” is an action target. It may support an objective such as reducing severe labor risk, but the audit count does not prove that conditions improved.

Ownership should follow authority and capability. One accountable owner coordinates; contributing functions retain their responsibilities. Resources, milestones, dependencies and escalation should be explicit. A goal without budget, competence or decision rights is not an implementation plan.

The Melanie Kwok project interviews emphasize that ESG should be integrated across projects and governance rather than added as an afterthought. Her material is a practitioner perspective, not endorsement.

## Lesson 4 — Indicators that support decisions

Indicators simplify reality. A **leading indicator** monitors a condition or action expected to influence future performance: unresolved high-risk supplier findings, percentage of critical records validated or completion of preventive maintenance. A **lagging indicator** records an outcome after it occurs: injuries, verified incidents or actual emissions.

An **input** is a resource, an **activity** is work performed, an **output** is its immediate deliverable and an **outcome** is the change that follows. These distinctions prevent four common errors:

**ACTIVITY ≠ PERFORMANCE**  
**METRIC ≠ OUTCOME**  
**TARGET ≠ ACHIEVEMENT**  
**REPORTING ≠ IMPACT**

A good indicator has a definition, boundary, method, owner, frequency, source, quality control, threshold and decision response. It should be tested for perverse incentives. Measuring audit closure time could encourage superficial closure unless effectiveness is also reviewed.

Choose the denominator that represents the decision. “Incidents per employee,” “per hour worked” and “per site” answer different questions. Keep absolute and intensity measures together where growth could hide total change. Pair leading and lagging measures, and do not label an output as impact without evidence of the causal pathway, baseline, counterfactual and contribution. If the metric creates incentives to suppress reporting, redesign the measure and its consequences.

## Lesson 5 — Integrate into existing processes

ESG becomes operational when embedded in processes that already shape decisions:

- strategy and portfolio review;
- capital allocation and business cases;
- requirements and design reviews;
- supplier selection, contracting and performance;
- project gates and change control;
- facility and operational planning;
- workforce, training and incentive systems;
- risk, compliance, audit and management review.

A parallel ESG approval can create duplication and ambiguity. Integration identifies where a relevant question, evidence field, control or authority belongs in the existing process. Specialist review remains necessary where competence is required.

## Lesson 6 — Project lifecycle integration

At concept stage, define affected systems, people and alternatives. In requirements, convert priorities into testable conditions. During sourcing and design, evaluate lifecycle and value-chain evidence. At approval gates, present uncertainty and authority. During delivery, monitor leading indicators and exceptions. At acceptance, verify evidence and controls. During operation, compare actual outcomes with assumptions. At end-of-life, address recovery, disposal and social consequences.

Late ESG review limits choices. Discovering material provenance or accessibility needs after design freeze can make change costly. Early integration does not mean resolving every issue immediately; it means identifying when evidence and decisions are needed.

## Lesson 7 — Capability, culture and change

Implementation changes routines and incentives. People need to know not only the requirement but why it matters, how to act and where to escalate. Capability can include technical methods, stakeholder engagement, data quality, supplier dialogue and ethical judgment.

Change management considers sponsorship, role clarity, training, tools, workload, feedback and reinforcement. If ESG tasks are added without removing conflicting demands, teams may produce surface compliance. Leaders must align targets and consequences so schedule, cost, quality and ESG evidence are managed together.

Resistance can reveal unclear purpose, impractical design or genuine disagreement. It should be examined rather than dismissed. The course does not require every concern to be accepted as fact; it requires an evidence-based response.

## Lesson 8 — Monitoring, review and corrective action

Monitoring asks whether actions occurred, controls operated and conditions changed. Review compares results with objectives and assumptions. Corrective action addresses causes, not only missed numbers. Learning updates methods, priorities and resources.

Transparency includes limits. A strategy should state uncertain data, dependencies, trade-offs and where outcomes are not yet known. This makes claims narrower but decisions stronger.

## Lesson 9 — Baselines, counterfactuals and attribution

An outcome cannot be interpreted without a reference. A baseline describes the starting condition. A counterfactual describes what might have happened without the intervention or under another option. Both can be uncertain.

If supplier incidents decline after a programme, the programme may have helped—but demand, supplier mix or reporting behavior may also have changed. If energy intensity improves, production volume or outsourcing may explain part of the result. Strategy review should avoid claiming sole attribution when evidence supports only contribution.

Sensitivity analysis asks which assumptions could reverse the decision. Scenario ranges are often more honest than one precise forecast. The aim is not to eliminate uncertainty; it is to know which uncertainty matters and what evidence should trigger change.

## Lesson 10 — Portfolio coherence and trade-offs

Individual projects can appear successful while the portfolio moves in the wrong direction. Efficiency improvements may be outweighed by growth; supplier controls may improve in one category while urgent sourcing expands elsewhere. Strategy should examine aggregate direction and interactions.

Trade-offs should be explicit. A project may improve readiness and reduce material use while increasing short-term freight. Another may strengthen worker evidence while raising cost and lead time. Some criteria are constraints; others are choices. Severe rights or mandatory technical failures should not be hidden inside averaged benefits.

Coherence also means testing commitments against capital, purchasing and incentive decisions. If budgets and requirements contradict the stated objective, reporting cannot repair the strategy.

## What this means in Air Power Services

### AIR POWER SERVICES LEARNING SCENARIO — Responsible sourcing becomes a managed objective

A fictional programme starts with “Improve responsible sourcing.” The team defines scope as high-criticality specialist materials and outsourced treatments for a retrofit. It identifies mineral provenance, severe labor risk, pollution controls and continuity as connected issues. It assigns a procurement owner with technical, quality, compliance and sustainability contributors. Actions include tier mapping, evidence requirements, purchasing-practice review and exception escalation. Indicators distinguish mapping activity, evidence quality, severe-risk response and verified improvement.

## Myth vs reality

### Myth 1: “An ESG strategy is a list of targets.”

**Reality:** Strategy connects priorities, choices, resources, ownership, actions, evidence and review.

### Myth 2: “More KPIs mean better control.”

**Reality:** Indicators must support decisions. Too many weak metrics can obscure material performance.

### Myth 3: “ESG needs a separate process for every decision.”

**Reality:** Most ESG questions should be integrated into existing governed processes, with specialist support where needed.

## Decision lens

1. What defined issue—not slogan—are we addressing?
2. What is in scope, who is affected and what evidence supports priority?
3. What outcome do we seek, and what constraints apply?
4. Who owns the objective and has authority/resources?
5. Which actions have a plausible causal link to outcome?
6. Which leading/lagging and output/outcome indicators support decisions?
7. Where does this belong in an existing process or project gate?
8. What uncertainty, trade-off or incentive could undermine it?
9. What threshold triggers review, escalation or corrective action?

## Module close

Implementation converts material issues into governed choices and learning. It succeeds when ESG evidence changes ordinary business processes rather than becoming a parallel reporting exercise.

### Key takeaways

- Materiality is a documented, revisable judgment; formal reporting assessments require the complete applicable framework and specialist governance.
- Define the issue before selecting a target.
- Activities, metrics, targets, reporting and outcomes are different.
- Ownership, evidence, capability and incentives determine execution.
- Monitoring should test assumptions and trigger correction.
