# ESG Essentials — Course Blueprint

**Course:** Environmental, Social & Governance for Air Power Services  
**Subtitle:** From Principles to Better Business Decisions  
**Audience:** Air Power Services employees with no prior ESG expertise  
**Planned duration:** 435 minutes (7 hours 15 minutes), excluding optional references  
**Architecture:** 10 modules, 43 substantive lessons, 10 formative checks, one cumulative decision lab

## Course-level learning outcomes

By the end of the course, learners will be able to:

1. Explain why ESG exists and distinguish impact, dependency, risk, opportunity, compliance and ethical responsibility.
2. Identify material environmental, social and governance issues in an Air Power Services decision.
3. interpret common climate, nature, social, finance, rating and reporting evidence without treating a metric or label as proof by itself.
4. Connect ESG issues to engineering, maintenance, procurement, supply-chain, project, operational and management decisions.
5. Recognize greenwashing, weak social evidence and governance/control failures.
6. Apply a repeatable evidence-based decision process, document trade-offs and escalate appropriately.

## Pedagogical spine

Each module follows a consistent sequence: **orient → build theory → inspect evidence → translate to Air Power → decide → receive feedback → check understanding**. Aerospace scenarios are original additions and will be labeled `APS original`; they do not alter or masquerade as supplied-source examples.

## 00 — ESG: Why It Exists (35 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Define ESG and the role of each pillar.
- Explain ESG as a decision lens for long-term value, impacts and stakeholder accountability—not a slogan or three independent checklists.
- Distinguish compliance, ethics, risk management and opportunity creation.
- Recognize how governance failures can amplify environmental and social harm.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **The problem ESG is trying to solve (6 min):** business within environmental and social systems; externalities; stakeholder expectations; long time horizons.
2. **Three pillars, one operating system (7 min):** E dependencies/impacts, S relationships/rights, G direction/control; cross-pillar interactions.
3. **From impact to business consequence (7 min):** impact, dependency, risk and opportunity; introductory materiality; two-way relationship between enterprise and society/environment.
4. **Compliance is a floor (5 min):** why legal compliance, ethical responsibility and strategic resilience overlap but are not identical.
5. **Case anatomy: Enron (7 min):** incentives, opaque structures, auditor conflict, silenced dissent, weak challenge, cascading consequences.
6. **Air Power relevance (3 min):** reliability, evidence, configuration control, supplier trust and lifecycle choices as familiar bridges to ESG thinking.

### Sources

1.2.1; 1.2.2; 1.2.3; 1.3.1; 3.1.6; 5.1.2 (exact filenames in `SOURCE_MAP.md`).

### Visual explainers

- Three interlocking system rings—environment, people, control—with “decision quality” at the center.
- Impact–dependency–risk–opportunity causal chain.
- Enron control-failure cascade, rendered as a neutral governance diagram (no speaker imagery).

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

A component sourcing decision appears technically acceptable and contractually compliant, but supplier evidence, worker conditions, material provenance and end-of-life handling are uncertain. Learners locate E/S/G connections before seeing terminology.

### Interactive activity

**“Which lens is missing?”** Sort ten decision signals into impact, dependency, risk, opportunity, control or stakeholder; multi-label answers are permitted and explained.

### Knowledge check

Five scenario-based questions. At least two require selecting multiple pillars and explaining why “compliant” does not automatically mean “responsible” or “resilient.”

## 01 — Environmental Essentials (60 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Explain climate stability, GHG Scopes 1–3, natural capital, biodiversity and circularity from first principles.
- Identify physical, transition, liability/reputation and supply risks.
- Use lifecycle and systems thinking to avoid burden shifting.
- Recognize uncertainty and evidence needs in environmental claims.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **Climate system and business exposure (8 min):** greenhouse effect at essentials level; anthropogenic change; mitigation vs adaptation; physical and transition risk.
2. **Carbon accounting (10 min):** organizational/value-chain boundaries; Scopes 1, 2 and 3; activity data, emission factors, estimates, double counting and decision usefulness.
3. **Targets and transition pathways (7 min):** baseline, boundary, target year, absolute vs intensity targets, residual emissions and credible transition plans.
4. **Natural capital and dependencies (8 min):** renewable/non-renewable resources, ecosystem services, planetary limits and location-specific dependency.
5. **Water, land, forests and minerals (8 min):** scarcity, degradation, extraction, community impacts and responsible sourcing.
6. **Biodiversity and nature risk (7 min):** ecosystem/species/genetic diversity; drivers of loss; dependencies, impacts and conservation hierarchy.
7. **Waste, circularity and lifecycle trade-offs (9 min):** refuse/reduce/reuse/repair/remanufacture/recycle; material trade-offs; avoiding single-metric optimization.
8. **Air Power synthesis (3 min):** connect energy, materials, chemicals, spares and infrastructure to operational decisions.

### Sources

1.2.1; 2.1; 2.2; 2.3.1–2.3.7; 2.4; 2.5; 3.3.3.

### Visual explainers

- Scope 1–2–3 boundary diagram built around a maintenance facility and upstream/downstream value chain.
- Climate risk chain: hazard × exposure × vulnerability → operational consequence.
- Natural-capital dependency/impact matrix.
- Circular lifecycle loop with leakage points and evidence gates.

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

- Compare repair, remanufacture and replacement of a high-value component using safety, readiness, energy, virgin material, waste and evidence criteria.
- Trace alloy/mineral provenance and processing impacts without claiming a material is inherently “green.”
- Facility water/energy resilience and hazardous-waste handling in maintenance operations.

### Interactive activity

**Lifecycle trade-off simulator (concept design):** learners adjust service life, mass, repairability, scrap recovery, energy source and supplier distance. The interface reveals burden shifts; it does not produce a false single “green score.”

### Knowledge check

Eight items: scope classification, mitigation/adaptation, risk type, circular hierarchy, biodiversity dependency and two short evidence critiques. Partial credit for defensible reasoning.

## 02 — Sustainable Finance & ESG Investment (45 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish sustainable, green, environmental, climate, impact and responsible-finance concepts.
- Explain how ESG factors may affect cash flow, company-specific risk and systemic risk.
- Compare exclusion, integration, engagement and thematic/impact approaches.
- Connect capital allocation and internal business cases to non-finance roles.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **Finance vocabulary without mystique (7 min):** umbrella/subset relationships; evolving terminology; products versus decision processes.
2. **Material ESG information in finance (8 min):** sector/business-model relevance; financial materiality; impact perspective; time horizon.
3. **How ESG can travel into value (8 min):** cash-flow, idiosyncratic-risk and systematic-risk channels; correlation is not automatic causation.
4. **Investment approaches (8 min):** values-based exclusions, ESG integration, best-in-class, stewardship/engagement, thematic and impact investing.
5. **Labeled instruments and use of proceeds (6 min):** green/social/sustainability bonds; eligibility, allocation, reporting and external review at concept level.
6. **From investment logic to project choices (8 min):** capital requests, total cost, resilience, avoided risk, uncertainty and non-financial outcomes.

### Sources

3.1.1–3.1.7; 3.2; 3.3.1–3.3.3; 3.4.2; 3.5.2.

### Visual explainers

- Finance-term nesting map with explicit “terminology varies” caveat.
- ESG-to-financial-performance transmission-channel map.
- Exclude–select–engage–fund-impact decision spectrum.

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

Build a business case for an energy-efficiency retrofit and a repair-capability investment. Learners compare upfront cost, readiness, resource use, supply resilience, measurement plan and rebound risk.

### Interactive activity

**Investment committee:** allocate a constrained budget across four projects. Each option exposes assumptions and measurement limitations after selection; no option is perfect.

### Knowledge check

Six questions that distinguish product labels from underlying evidence and require learners to choose an investment/engagement approach for different objectives.

## 03 — Ratings, Reporting & Greenwashing (45 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Explain what ESG ratings and disclosures can and cannot establish.
- Diagnose why ratings differ.
- Distinguish reporting standards, frameworks, metrics, taxonomies and ratings at concept level.
- Apply a structured anti-greenwashing test to claims and evidence.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **Why organizations report (6 min):** accountability, investor/contract/customer information, management control and comparability.
2. **The reporting architecture (8 min):** boundary, material topics, metrics, controls, narrative, governance, assurance; standards vs frameworks vs taxonomies.
3. **How ESG ratings work (8 min):** data collection, issue selection, weighting, peer comparison and score aggregation.
4. **Why ratings disagree (7 min):** scope, measurement, weights, data, controversy treatment and purpose; rating as model output, not truth.
5. **Greenwashing anatomy (9 min):** vague, selective, irrelevant, unsupported, boundary-shifting and future-only claims; fraud distinguished from misleading sustainability characterization.
6. **Evidence and assurance (7 min):** claim–boundary–baseline–method–data–control–owner–outcome; reasonable skepticism and escalation.

### Sources

3.1.4; 3.3.4; 3.4.1–3.4.4; 3.5.1–3.5.2; 3.7.1; 5.2.3.

### Visual explainers

- “Same company, different rating” model-comparison diagram.
- Reporting data pipeline with ownership and control points.
- Eight-part claim-evidence chain and red-flag overlay.

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

Evaluate three claims: “low-carbon repair,” “responsibly sourced alloy,” and “zero-waste maintenance line.” Learners find missing boundaries, counterfactuals, exclusions, controls and outcome evidence.

### Interactive activity

**Evidence inspector:** progressively reveal a supplier claim, methodology note, dataset and assurance statement. Learners update confidence and record what would change the decision.

### Knowledge check

Seven items including a rating-divergence diagnosis, reporting-term match and two greenwashing red-flag cases. Feedback reinforces uncertainty rather than rewarding cynicism.

## 04 — Social Essentials: People & Equity (45 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Explain the social pillar through human rights, fair work, safety, equity, inclusion, accessibility and stakeholder relationships.
- Distinguish equality from equity and diversity from inclusion/belonging.
- Recognize intersectionality, power and barriers in workplace systems.
- Respond constructively to DEI uncertainty or pushback while respecting evidence and local law.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **Who is affected? (6 min):** employees, contractors, customers/users, communities and value-chain workers; rights holders vs interested stakeholders.
2. **Human rights and decent work foundations (7 min):** dignity, non-discrimination, safe work, voice, privacy and remedy; company responsibility at concept level.
3. **Diversity, equity, inclusion and belonging (8 min):** precise definitions, substantive vs formal equality, accessibility and intersectionality.
4. **Systems, not symbolism (8 min):** recruitment, progression, pay, allocation of opportunity, accommodations, psychological safety and data governance.
5. **Measurement and limitations (7 min):** representation, experience, process and outcome measures; small populations, privacy and misleading averages.
6. **Dialogue, resistance and localization (6 min):** principled purpose, respectful conversation, evidence, cultural context and non-retaliation.
7. **Civil society and trust (3 min):** why external perspectives can reveal blind spots.

### Sources

1.2.2; 4.1.1; 4.2.1–4.2.6; 4.2.9–4.2.10; 4.3.3.

### Visual explainers

- Rights-holder/stakeholder orbit map.
- Equality–equity–accessibility decision sequence (without simplistic “boxes at a fence” imagery).
- DEI measurement stack: inputs, processes, experience, outcomes and governance.

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

- Fair allocation of training, shifts, developmental assignments and certification pathways.
- Inclusive maintenance instructions, PPE/equipment fit, accessibility and human-factors design.
- Psychological safety to report errors, hazards and ethical concerns.

### Interactive activity

**Barrier walkthrough:** inspect a promotion/training process and identify where apparently neutral rules create unequal access. Learners propose controls, measures and a feedback channel.

### Knowledge check

Six scenario items; no identity trivia. Assessment focuses on systems, respectful conduct, privacy, evidence and escalation.

## 05 — Social Risk in Global Supply Chains (50 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Identify human-rights and labor-risk drivers in multi-tier supply chains.
- Explain how purchasing practices and subcontracting can create or amplify vulnerability.
- Evaluate supplier evidence beyond certifications and pass/fail audits.
- Distinguish detection, prevention, mitigation, remediation and disengagement.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **Why risk hides beyond tier one (7 min):** opacity, labor intermediaries, informal work, migration, high-risk geographies/processes and commercial pressure.
2. **Severe social risks (9 min):** forced labor, trafficking, debt bondage, recruitment fees, child labor, unsafe work, discrimination and suppression of voice—handled non-graphically.
3. **Purchasing practice as a causal factor (7 min):** price, lead time, forecast volatility, late changes and payment terms.
4. **Risk-based due diligence (9 min):** map, assess, prioritize, act, track, communicate, enable remedy; severity and likelihood.
5. **What social audits reveal—and miss (8 min):** announced visits, document fraud, coached workers, sampling, conflict and snapshot limitations.
6. **Evidence triangulation and worker voice (6 min):** grievance channels, interviews, recruitment records, payroll/time records, corrective-action closure and trusted civil society.
7. **Response and remedy (4 min):** protect affected people, correct root causes, avoid abrupt disengagement that worsens harm, escalate governance.

### Sources

4.3.3; 4.4.2–4.4.4; 2.3.5; 3.3.2; 3.3.3.

### Visual explainers

- Multi-tier aerospace supply network with labor agents and high-risk transformation steps.
- Due-diligence cycle with remedy at the center.
- Evidence-triangulation triangle: documents, observation and worker voice.

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

Trace a specialist alloy/component through processor, sub-tier mine/refiner, labor recruiter and logistics provider. Procurement signals include repeated overtime, recruitment debt, inconsistent payroll, audit coaching and implausibly low pricing.

### Interactive activity

**Supplier evidence board:** learners weigh audit reports, grievance data, corrective-action history, worker interviews and procurement conditions, then choose continue/improve, pause/escalate or responsible disengagement.

### Knowledge check

Eight questions prioritize severity, vulnerable groups, causal purchasing practices, audit limitations and remedy. One branching item tests whether the learner protects people before protecting the metric.

## 06 — Governance Essentials (40 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Explain governance as the system for direction, oversight, accountability and control.
- Distinguish board, executive, management, risk, compliance, audit and operational responsibilities.
- Connect culture, incentives, controls, information and speak-up mechanisms.
- Recognize common governance failure patterns.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **Governance is how commitments become decisions (6 min):** purpose, decision rights, accountability and stakeholder interests.
2. **The three lines model at concept level (7 min):** ownership/operation, risk/compliance oversight and independent assurance; avoid implying a single mandated structure.
3. **Risk management and internal control (8 min):** objective–risk–control–evidence–assurance; preventive/detective/corrective controls.
4. **Information and escalation (6 min):** accurate reporting, thresholds, bad-news flow, whistleblowing and non-retaliation.
5. **Culture, incentives and ethics (6 min):** conduct shaped by rewards, pressure, example and consequence management.
6. **Failure patterns (7 min):** domination, conflicts, opacity, control override, fragmented accountability and normalization of deviance.

### Sources

1.2.3; 1.3.1; 5.1.1–5.1.4; 3.2.

### Visual explainers

- Governance accountability map from board to frontline control owner.
- Objective–risk–control–evidence chain.
- Enron failure bow-tie: drivers, failed barriers and consequences.

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

Map governance for a maintenance-process change: technical authority, operational owner, quality, safety, procurement, ESG subject matter, approval body, evidence repository and escalation thresholds.

### Interactive activity

**Control-room challenge:** match twelve controls to risks, owners and evidence; identify one control that exists on paper but is not operating effectively.

### Knowledge check

Six items on accountability, control type, independence, conflicts, culture and escalation. Distractors distinguish responsibility from assurance.

## 07 — Boards, Oversight & Ethical Decisions (40 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Explain board composition, independence, expertise and committee oversight.
- Identify conflicts and conditions that weaken constructive challenge.
- Apply an ethical decision framework under uncertainty and pressure.
- Describe what decision-ready ESG information should reach senior oversight.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **What boards do—and do not do (6 min):** direction, oversight, executive accountability and reserved decisions vs operational management.
2. **Composition and independence (7 min):** skills, diversity, tenure, conflicts, information access and independent challenge.
3. **Committees and assurance (6 min):** audit, risk, remuneration/people, nomination and sustainability arrangements; structure follows context.
4. **Decision-ready information (6 min):** materiality, trend, uncertainty, control effectiveness, incidents, stakeholder impact and options.
5. **Ethical decision protocol (9 min):** facts/unknowns, affected parties and rights, duties and principles, consequences, alternatives, reversibility, transparency and escalation.
6. **Mission and stakeholder governance (6 min):** compare corporate and non-profit governance to reveal transferable principles and limits.

### Sources

4.2.5; 5.1.4; 5.2.2–5.2.4; 1.3.1.

### Visual explainers

- Board–committee–management boundary map.
- “Challenge quality” conditions wheel.
- Ethical decision canvas with explicit uncertainty field.

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

Senior review of a readiness-critical supplier change where technical compliance is documented but labor evidence and long-term material availability are weak. Learners prepare a one-page escalation view.

### Interactive activity

**Board packet triage:** choose which facts, trends, uncertainties and control failures must be escalated; reject vanity metrics and unsupported assurances.

### Knowledge check

Six questions on independence, conflicts, information sufficiency, board/management boundaries and defensible ethical reasoning.

## 08 — ESG into Business Strategy (40 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Integrate ESG into strategy, portfolio and project lifecycles.
- Prioritize material issues using impact, dependency, risk/opportunity and stakeholder evidence.
- Set decision-useful objectives, indicators, controls and ownership.
- Manage cross-pillar trade-offs transparently.

### Detailed lesson structure and theory

1. **From topics to material decisions (7 min):** context, value chain, stakeholders, impacts/dependencies, risks/opportunities and prioritization.
2. **Strategy choices and transition plans (7 min):** ambition, pathways, resources, dependencies, assumptions and governance.
3. **Project lifecycle integration (8 min):** concept, requirements, sourcing, design, delivery, acceptance, operation and end-of-life gates.
4. **Metrics that support decisions (6 min):** leading/lagging, input/output/outcome, absolute/intensity, thresholds and perverse incentives.
5. **Controls, assurance and learning (6 min):** ownership, data lineage, change control, review, corrective action and lessons learned.
6. **Trade-offs and just transition (6 min):** safety/readiness as constraints; avoid harm transfer among workers, communities, regions or generations.

### Sources

3.2; 3.3.1–3.3.3; 3.4.4; 3.7.1; 4.2.2–4.2.3; 4.3.3; 4.4.4; 5.1.4; 5.2.3; 6.1.3–6.1.5.

### Visual explainers

- Materiality funnel from context to governed priority.
- Project lifecycle with ESG questions/evidence at each gate.
- Metric architecture: objective → indicator → threshold → action → owner → assurance.

### Aerospace translation (`APS original`)

Build an ESG requirements/evidence plan for a retrofit programme: technical safety, readiness, energy, material traceability, workforce capability, supplier rights, lifecycle waste, data ownership and decision governance.

### Interactive activity

**Strategy map builder:** connect a material issue to objective, action, KPI, control, owner, stakeholder, decision threshold and review cadence. The system flags missing causal links.

### Knowledge check

Six integrated items that test prioritization, metric quality, lifecycle placement, trade-offs and governance—not recall of framework acronyms.

## 09 — Air Power Services ESG Decision Lab (35 minutes)

### Learning objectives

- Apply the full ESG decision process to an ambiguous Air Power Services scenario.
- Select proportionate evidence, identify assumptions and record uncertainty.
- Balance safety/readiness, cost, environmental performance, human impacts and governance.
- Produce an auditable recommendation and escalation plan.

### Scenario

`APS original`: A fleet-support programme must address obsolescence in a component family. Options include continued repair, redesign/retrofit, new manufacture through an incumbent supplier, or rapid onboarding of a lower-cost supplier. Evidence is incomplete across lifecycle emissions, alloy provenance, labor recruitment, waste recovery, technical capacity, delivery resilience and control maturity. No option dominates every dimension.

### Detailed lab sequence

1. **Mission brief and constraints (4 min).**
2. **Stakeholder, dependency and impact map (5 min).**
3. **Material risk/opportunity screen (5 min).**
4. **Evidence-room investigation (7 min).**
5. **Options and trade-off analysis (5 min).**
6. **Controls, conditions and escalation (4 min).**
7. **Recommendation and confidence statement (3 min).**
8. **Expert-model debrief and transfer plan (2 min).**

### Sources

All modules, anchored especially in 2.3.5, 2.4, 3.2, 3.3.4, 4.4.2–4.4.4, 5.1.4, 5.2.3 and 6.1.3–6.1.5.

### Visual explainers

- Technical digital-twin decision board (abstract interface, not a false operational system).
- Multi-criteria option matrix with uncertainty and veto constraints shown separately.
- Evidence provenance trail.

### Interactive activity and assessment

This module is the cumulative branching assessment. Learners may revisit evidence, request clarification, reject unsupported claims and condition an approval. Scoring rubric:

- 25% material issue identification
- 20% evidence quality and uncertainty
- 20% cross-pillar/system reasoning
- 15% protection of safety, rights and non-negotiable controls
- 10% governance/escalation
- 10% clarity and traceability of recommendation

Completion requires 75%, but the debrief explains trade-offs and allows one targeted retry. No hidden “perfect green answer” exists.

## Assessment architecture

- Formative checks provide explanatory feedback and allow retries.
- At least 60% of check items are scenario/application questions.
- Acronym recall is never a learning objective unless the acronym is operationally necessary.
- Question banks use controlled variants without changing the construct measured.
- Final lab scores reasoning dimensions separately; safety, rights and integrity controls cannot be traded away for aggregate points.
- Completion data should store module/check status, not sensitive DEI responses or free-text ethical reflections.
