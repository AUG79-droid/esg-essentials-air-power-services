# ESG Essentials — Terminology Standard

## Production rule

These are the approved learner-facing meanings for the frozen course. Use plain-language definitions on first meaningful use, then use the preferred term consistently. A module may add context, but it must not redefine the term. Capitalize Environmental, Social and Governance when naming the three ESG dimensions; otherwise use normal sentence case. Use **Air Power Services Learning Scenario** for every fictional APS example.

| Term | Approved learner-facing meaning | Preferred usage / boundary |
|---|---|---|
| ESG | A practical lens for considering environmental, social and governance information in decisions, oversight and accountability. | Never present ESG as a single score, approval authority, certification or synonym for sustainability. |
| sustainability | The broad objective of maintaining the environmental, social and organizational conditions needed for systems and activities to continue over time. | Broader than ESG. Do not use as an unsupported product attribute. |
| Environmental | How an organization depends on and affects climate, energy, water, land, materials, pollution, waste and living systems. | Not shorthand for “green” or automatically beneficial. |
| Social | How decisions affect people, rights, work, wellbeing, access and relationships across operations and value chains. | Not limited to DEI, philanthropy or employees. |
| Governance | The system of rules, roles, authority, practices and processes through which an organization is directed, controlled and held accountable. | Use lowercase governance for the general concept. Not limited to board composition. |
| impact | A change to people, society or the environment that an organization causes, contributes to or is connected with. | Do not use as a synonym for output, outcome, risk or financial effect. Context must identify positive/negative and actual/potential where relevant. |
| dependency | An environmental or social condition, resource, service or relationship on which an activity or organization relies. | Keep distinct from impact; a topic can be both. |
| risk | Uncertainty that could affect objectives or produce adverse consequences. | Specify the lens: risk to people/environment or risk to the organization. Do not collapse them. |
| opportunity | A condition or action that could improve performance, resilience or positive outcomes. | An opportunity does not offset a mandatory constraint or severe harm. |
| materiality | A structured, purpose-dependent judgment about which issues or information matter sufficiently for a decision or report. | Never imply one universal materiality test. State purpose, boundary, evidence and criteria. |
| impact materiality | Whether an organization’s actual or potential effects on people or the environment are significant. | Severity is central for negative impacts; likelihood informs potential impacts. |
| financial materiality | Whether sustainability-related risks or opportunities could affect organizational prospects and the decisions of relevant financial-information users. | Do not equate with immediate cost only. |
| double materiality | Consideration of impact materiality and financial materiality as related but distinct lenses. | An issue may qualify through either lens. Do not call ISSB materiality “double materiality.” |
| stakeholder | A person or group with an interest in, influence on or potential effect from a decision. | Broader than rights-holder. Interests are not all equivalent to rights. |
| rights-holder | A person or group whose individual or collective rights may be affected. | Preferred hyphenated form. Do not reduce rights to survey preferences. |
| evidence | Information that supports, challenges or limits a judgment or claim, with an identifiable source and scope. | Distinguish measured, estimated, reported, alleged, inferred and unknown. |
| claim | A statement presented for an audience that must be supportable within its wording, boundary, period and evidence. | Never treat a label, target or certificate as complete proof. |
| metric | A defined measure produced by a stated method, source, boundary and frequency. | A metric is not automatically a target, outcome or impact. |
| target | A defined future level or condition to be achieved within a boundary and time horizon. | A target is not achievement or outcome. |
| input | A resource used for an activity. | Examples: funding, people, equipment or time. |
| activity | Work performed. | Examples: training delivered or audit conducted. Not proof of performance. |
| output | The immediate deliverable produced by an activity. | Examples: completed assessment or trained participants. Distinct from outcome. |
| outcome | A change in condition or behavior following actions and outputs. | Do not call an activity or output an outcome. |
| baseline | The defined reference condition or period against which change is assessed. | State method and any restatement. Distinct from counterfactual. |
| boundary | What is included and excluded from a measure, assessment, claim or decision. | State organizational, value-chain, geographic, temporal and lifecycle boundaries as relevant. |
| uncertainty | A limitation in knowledge, data, model, assumption or future condition that could affect interpretation or decision. | Do not use as a reason to ignore risk or claim false precision. |
| verification | Checking specified information, performance or conformity against defined requirements. | Do not use interchangeably with certification, internal review or external assurance. |
| assurance | An independent engagement that evaluates information against suitable criteria and provides a formal conclusion under an applicable standard. | Always state scope and level where known. Readiness is not assurance. |
| internal review | Management or peer challenge of work, evidence or conclusions. | Not independent external assurance. |
| internal audit | Independent assurance and advisory activity within the organization, operating under its mandate. | Management retains ownership of operations, risks and controls. |
| control | An action or mechanism designed to modify risk, support objectives or ensure a requirement is met. | A policy alone is not necessarily an operating control. Separate design, execution and effectiveness. |
| risk capacity | The maximum risk an organization could bear without losing viability or the ability to meet mandatory obligations. | Use only where the selected framework adopts the term. |
| risk appetite | The type and amount of risk an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of objectives. | Does not authorize violation of mandatory constraints. |
| risk tolerance | The acceptable variation or exposure for a specific objective or risk category, translating appetite into a more specific range. | Framework definitions vary; state the adopted definition. |
| risk limit | An operational boundary or trigger used to prevent, escalate or correct exposure outside tolerance. | A breached mandatory limit cannot be renamed after the fact. |
| lifecycle thinking | Checking impacts and trade-offs across relevant stages—from extraction through production, use, maintenance and end of life—to avoid burden shifting. | Preferred closed compound “lifecycle” in general prose; not a formal LCA. |
| LCA | A formal life-cycle assessment using defined goal/scope, functional unit, system boundary, inventory, impact assessment and interpretation. | Write “life-cycle assessment (LCA)” on first use. Do not imply course completion qualifies an LCA practitioner. |
| due diligence | An ongoing, risk-based process to identify, assess, prioritise, prevent or mitigate, track and communicate impacts, and enable remedy where appropriate. | Not a one-time audit or guarantee. Specify human-rights or responsible-business context. |
| remedy | Action that addresses the effects of harm on affected people. | Distinct from stopping harm, preventing recurrence and correcting a process. Do not invent legal remedies. |
| mitigation | Action that reduces the drivers, likelihood or severity of an adverse effect. | In climate content, mitigation reduces climate-change drivers; in impact/risk content, name what is being reduced. |
| adaptation | Adjustment that reduces exposure or vulnerability to actual or expected climate effects. | Distinct from climate mitigation. |
| resilience | Capacity to cope, adapt, learn or reorganize while maintaining essential function. | Do not claim resilience from one control or metric alone. |
| escalation | Deliberate transfer of an issue to an authority able and required to decide or act when thresholds, evidence, competence or authority are exceeded. | Not failure or avoidance; include trigger, destination and timing. |
| decision authority | The role or body formally empowered to make a specified decision. | Participation, advice, review or commercial onboarding do not imply decision authority. |
| technical authority | The competent authority for mandatory technical decisions, including applicable safety and airworthiness decisions. | ESG never substitutes for or overrides it. |

## Terms that must not be used interchangeably

- ESG ≠ sustainability.
- Environmental benefit ≠ overall sustainability.
- Dependency ≠ impact ≠ risk ≠ opportunity.
- Risk to people/environment ≠ risk to the organization.
- Materiality ≠ importance in ordinary speech; impact materiality ≠ financial materiality.
- Stakeholder ≠ rights-holder.
- Evidence ≠ claim; certificate ≠ complete evidence.
- Metric ≠ target ≠ output ≠ outcome ≠ impact.
- Baseline ≠ counterfactual.
- Verification ≠ certification ≠ assurance ≠ internal review ≠ internal audit.
- Policy ≠ procedure ≠ control; control execution ≠ control effectiveness.
- Risk capacity ≠ appetite ≠ tolerance ≠ limit.
- Lifecycle thinking ≠ formal LCA.
- Prevention/mitigation/corrective action ≠ remedy.
- Climate mitigation ≠ adaptation ≠ resilience.
- Communication/notification ≠ consultation/engagement.
- Commercial onboarding ≠ technical approval ≠ ESG assurance.
- Completion of the learning experience ≠ certification, qualification, competence or corporate authorization.
